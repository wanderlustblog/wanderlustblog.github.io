How Accurate Is Google Maps Travel Time, Really?

August 21, 20268 min readby WanderLust Blog

Google Maps said forty minutes. It took just over two hours, most of it behind a stalled truck on a single-lane mountain road with nowhere to pass. We've learned to read its estimates a little differently since.

In cities, Google Maps' travel time estimates are genuinely impressive — pulling live traffic data from millions of phones to adjust arrival times almost in real time. The further you get from that density of data, though, the more the estimate starts to feel like a rough guess rather than a prediction, and we've learned exactly where that shift tends to happen.

Where the estimates hold up well

Anywhere with heavy phone usage and well-mapped roads — major highways, city centers, popular tourist corridors — Google Maps' travel time is usually close enough to plan around confidently. It's factoring in live congestion from other users' phones, which means the busier and more "digitally visible" a route is, the better the estimate tends to be.

Where it quietly falls apart

Rural roads and mountain routes are where the gap shows up most. Riding Sri Lanka's hill-country train through the tea plantations, we noticed the app's estimated arrival time bore little resemblance to the train's actual schedule — not because the app was wrong exactly, but because it simply wasn't built to track a single-track rail line with its own quirks and delays. Anywhere transport depends on something other than a car on a mapped road, treat the estimate as a loose guess.

Border crossings and informal delays

Crossing into new territory rarely shows up in the estimate at all. On the drive through Georgia's mountain routes toward the border, what looked like a twenty-minute stretch on the map turned into well over an hour once checkpoint queues and unpaved detours entered the picture — delays that simply don't exist in any traffic model, however good.

Road surface matters more than the map admits

Speed estimates generally assume a road behaves the way its classification suggests — a "major road" moves at major-road speeds. In practice, potholes, seasonal washouts, and stretches under informal repair can slow a technically-major road to a crawl, something we ran into more than once navigating toward rural stretches of Vietnam, where the map's confident time estimate assumed a road quality that didn't quite match what we found on arrival.

A simple buffer rule that's saved us more than once

For any route we haven't personally driven before — especially rural, mountainous, or cross-border — we now pad Google's estimate by roughly 30 to 50 percent before committing to a same-day plan around it. It's a rough rule rather than a precise one, but it's kept us from missing flights, ferries, and check-in windows more times than we can count. For general logistics and flight-side planning where the data tends to be much more reliable, Google's travel tools remain genuinely useful — it's specifically the on-the-ground rural drive time where the healthy skepticism belongs.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Google Maps travel time sometimes way off?

Estimates rely on historical and live traffic data, which thins out fast in rural or less-mapped areas, and they can't account for unpaved roads, border crossings, or unpredictable local conditions.

Does Google Maps account for road quality?

Only loosely — it factors in road type and speed limits where known, but potholes, seasonal washouts, and informal detours generally aren't reflected.

Should I add extra time to Google Maps estimates when traveling abroad?

For rural routes, mountain roads, or border crossings, padding the estimate by 30-50% is a reasonable habit, especially in regions with less consistent mapping data.
