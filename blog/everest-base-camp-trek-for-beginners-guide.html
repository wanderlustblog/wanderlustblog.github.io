Everest Base Camp Trek for Beginners: Fitness, Route, Altitude, Permits and Season

October 28, 202614 min readby WanderLust Blog

You do not need to be a mountaineer to reach Everest Base Camp, but you do need to respect altitude, prepare your body and plan the days properly.

The Everest Base Camp trek is one of the world's best-known hikes, ranking among the top bucket-list experiences in recent traveller surveys. It follows well-established trails through Sherpa villages, suspension bridges and high mountain valleys to the foot of the world's highest mountain. It is a walking trek, not a climb, and many first-time trekkers complete it successfully. However, it takes you to a very high altitude where the air is thin and the weather is serious, so beginners should treat preparation, acclimatisation and safety as the most important parts of the trip. This guide covers the route, fitness, altitude, permits, seasons and packing.

Is the Everest Base Camp Trek Suitable for Beginners?

Yes, if you are reasonably fit, motivated to train and willing to go slowly. The trek involves walking five to nine hours a day on trails with steady ascents and descents, often at altitudes where even short climbs feel hard. You do not need technical climbing skills, ropes or crampons for the standard trek in the main seasons. The main challenge is altitude, followed by weather, basic accommodation and the physical effort of many consecutive days. If you have heart, lung or other medical conditions, speak to your doctor before booking.

The Standard Route and Itinerary

Most trekkers follow a classic route that takes about twelve to fourteen days, including two acclimatisation days.

  • Fly to Lukla and trek to Phakding: a short, scenic first day. The flight to Lukla's mountain airstrip is spectacular and weather dependent, so keep a buffer.
  • Phakding to Namche Bazaar: a long climb to the Sherpa capital, with suspension bridges and your first views of the high peaks.
  • Acclimatisation day at Namche: a short hike higher and a return to sleep low, to help your body adapt.
  • Namche to Tengboche and Dingboche: the trail continues past a famous monastery and into higher valleys.
  • Acclimatisation day at Dingboche: another climb-high, sleep-low day.
  • Dingboche to Lobuche and Gorak Shep: the final approach, with thin air and cold.
  • Base Camp and Kala Patthar: a visit to Base Camp itself, at about 5,364 metres, and a sunrise or sunset climb to Kala Patthar at about 5,545 metres, which gives the best views of Everest.
  • The descent: the return is much faster, usually in four to five days, ending with the flight back from Lukla.

Altitude: The Most Important Section

Altitude sickness can affect anyone, regardless of fitness. It occurs because there is less oxygen at height, and it is made worse by climbing too fast. Mild symptoms include headache, loss of appetite, nausea, trouble sleeping and dizziness. More serious signs include confusion, loss of balance, a persistent cough, shortness of breath at rest or a severe headache that does not respond to rest. If symptoms worsen, you must descend immediately and seek help.

  • Go slowly: do not rush, and walk at a pace where you can talk.
  • Climb high, sleep low: use the acclimatisation days, and do not skip them.
  • Limit the daily gain in sleeping altitude above about 3,000 metres. A guide will plan this for you.
  • Drink plenty of water and eat regularly, even if your appetite is low.
  • Avoid alcohol and sleeping pills at altitude.
  • Talk to a doctor before the trip about medication for altitude, and do not take any drugs without medical advice.
  • Tell your guide how you feel, honestly and early.

Do not ignore symptoms to reach Base Camp. Descending is always the right choice if you are unwell.

Fitness and Training Plan

Start training three to four months before the trek. Build a base of cardiovascular fitness with walking, running, cycling or swimming, and add hill walking with a loaded backpack to practise the actual activity. Strength training for legs and core helps with descents, and flexibility and balance work protect against injury. Aim to walk for several hours on consecutive days before you go, so your body is used to repeated effort. Break in your boots well before the trip, and practise with the gear you plan to carry.

Permits and Rules

Trekking in the Everest region requires permits, including an entry permit for Sagarmatha National Park and a local permit for the rural municipality. Rules and fees can change, and there have been announcements about requirements for guides, so check the latest official information from the Nepal tourism authorities and from your trekking company before you book. Most trekkers arrange permits through their agency. Carry your passport and copies of your permits, since checkpoints along the trail may ask for them.

Best Time to Go

  • March to May (spring): warmer, with rhododendrons in bloom. This is the main pre-monsoon season, and many climbers are heading to Everest, so trails and lodges are busy.
  • September to November (autumn): generally clear skies and stable weather after the monsoon. October is the most popular month.
  • December to February (winter): very cold, but with fewer crowds and often clear views. Some lodges close, and it needs more cold-weather gear and experience.
  • June to August (monsoon): rain, cloud and flight disruptions, with leeches in lower areas. It is not recommended for beginners.

Guides, Porters and Accommodation

Hiring a licensed guide is strongly advised for a first trek, as guides know the route, watch for altitude symptoms and help with logistics. A porter can carry your main bag, letting you hike with a light daypack, and it supports local employment. Choose companies that treat their staff fairly, provide proper clothing and insurance for porters and follow responsible practices. You will sleep in tea houses, simple lodges with basic rooms, shared bathrooms and a dining room heated by a stove. Bring a warm sleeping bag, since rooms are not heated.

What to Pack

  • Broken-in waterproof hiking boots, spare laces and warm, thick socks.
  • Layers: thermal base layers, a fleece, an insulated down jacket and a waterproof shell.
  • Warm hat, sun hat, gloves, sunglasses with strong UV protection and sunscreen.
  • A four-season sleeping bag, a daypack and trekking poles.
  • A water bottle, purification tablets or a filter, a head torch and spare batteries.
  • A basic first-aid kit and any personal medication, prescribed by your doctor.
  • A power bank, since charging can be slow and costly at high altitude.

Insurance and Safety

Travel insurance that covers high-altitude trekking up to at least 6,000 metres and helicopter evacuation is essential. Read the policy carefully, since many standard policies exclude trekking at altitude. Keep emergency contacts, your insurance details and your guide's contact information with you. Follow your guide's instructions, avoid trekking alone, and be careful with food and water hygiene to avoid stomach illness.

Budget Planning Without Guesswork

Costs depend on whether you go independently or with an agency and how much comfort you want. Build your estimate from international flights, domestic flights to Lukla, permits, guide and porter fees, accommodation, meals, equipment you need to buy or rent, insurance and a buffer of 15 percent or more. Compare quotes from several licensed companies, and check what is included. Prices are higher in peak season.

More Questions Beginners Ask

How hard is it really?

It is physically demanding but achievable with preparation. The altitude, not the technical difficulty, is the main challenge.

Can I do it without a guide?

Some experienced trekkers do, but a guide is strongly recommended for a first trek, and rules about guides may apply.

What if the Lukla flight is cancelled?

Weather often delays flights, so keep at least two spare days. Some alternative arrangements, such as flying from a different airport or taking a helicopter, may be possible but cost more.

Is the trek safe?

With good preparation and attention to altitude, many people complete it safely. Take warning signs seriously and be willing to turn back.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Skipping acclimatisation days to save time.
  • Not training enough or breaking in boots too late.
  • Booking a trip in the monsoon season without good reason.
  • Ignoring early altitude symptoms.
  • Buying cheap insurance that excludes high-altitude trekking.

Final Checklist

  • Medical check-up completed and a fitness plan followed for three to four months.
  • Licensed agency, permits and altitude-appropriate insurance arranged.
  • Broken-in boots, warm layers, sleeping bag and first-aid kit packed.
  • Spare days in your schedule for weather and acclimatisation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a beginner do the Everest Base Camp trek?

Yes, with good fitness, three to four months of training and a slow, acclimatised itinerary.

How long does the trek take?

Typically 12 to 14 days round trip, including acclimatisation days.

What is the best time to go?

March to May and September to November are the main seasons.

Do I need a permit?

Yes. Permits are required, and rules can change, so check official sources and your agency.

What is the biggest risk?

Altitude sickness. Go slowly, use acclimatisation days and descend if symptoms worsen.

Do I need a guide?

A licensed guide is strongly recommended, and rules about guides may apply.
