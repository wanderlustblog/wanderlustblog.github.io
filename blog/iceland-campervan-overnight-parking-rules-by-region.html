Iceland Campervan Overnight Parking Rules by Region: Where You Can Actually Sleep

November 24, 202617 min readby WanderLust Blog

The short answer: in Iceland you should plan to sleep in your campervan at an organised campsite, not at the roadside, in a car park or at a viewpoint. This guide explains the overnight parking rules region by region, what a night costs and how to plan every stop.

Almost everyone who dreams of an Iceland road trip pictures the same scene: a campervan parked beside a waterfall, the door open, a kettle on the stove, and not another soul in sight. It is a lovely picture, and it is also the picture that gets travellers into trouble. Iceland changed its camping rules in 2015, and today the answer to "can I just park here and sleep?" is usually no.

That does not mean the trip is any less magical. It means the nights are something you plan, and the plan is easier than it sounds. Below we explain what the law says in plain language, how the rules play out in each part of the country, where overnight stays are specifically banned, what campsites cost and how to avoid the mistakes we see most often. If you are still deciding on a route, our Iceland Ring Road story will give you ideas, and if something does go wrong on the road, our guide to an Iceland campervan breakdown and who to call covers the first steps.

Rules, prices and opening dates change, and we are not lawyers. Always confirm the details with the campsite, your rental company and official sources before you travel.

What the Law Says About Sleeping in a Campervan in Iceland

The rule comes from Iceland's Nature Conservation Act, no. 60/2013, which tightened in November 2015. In the Environment Agency's wording, it is illegal to spend the night in tent trailers, tent campers, caravans, camper vans or similar outside organised campsites or urban areas unless the landowner or rightholder has given permission. Three parts of that sentence matter.

  • "Outside organised campsites." A campsite is the safe, simple answer. If you sleep at one, you are following the rule.
  • "Urban areas." The Act treats built-up areas differently, but towns set their own local rules and the guidance for Reykjavik says sleeping in vehicles is not allowed within the city. Do not assume a town car park is a free overnight spot.
  • "Unless the landowner or rightholder has given permission." A farmer or landowner can say yes, but they are also entitled to say no. If they refuse, you move on.

One point trips people up more than any other: the difference between parking and sleeping. Sleeping in the vehicle is what counts as camping. Several guides note that leaving a vehicle parked overnight where other cars may park is a different matter from sleeping in it, but that is not a loophole for the roadside or a tourist car park. Stopping for photographs or the northern lights where parking is allowed is fine. Spending the night there is not.

And no sign does not mean permission. Rental-company guides stress that camping in a campervan outside a campsite is prohibited by law even when nothing is posted.

Campervans Versus Tents: Why the Rules Differ

You may have read that "wild camping is allowed in Iceland". That is true only in a narrow way, and only for people in tents. The same official page that restricts vehicles allows a traditional tent for one night on uncultivated land in inhabited areas, provided there is no campsite in the immediate vicinity and the landowner has not restricted access. In uninhabited areas, tents are allowed on private or national land more freely. The tent exceptions do not apply to campervans.

Iceland Campervan Overnight Parking Rules by Region

The national rule is the same everywhere, but what it means for your night changes with the landscape. Some regions are packed with campsites. Others have a handful, and a few of Iceland's best-known places ban overnight stays altogether. Start with the table, then open the region you are driving through.

Reykjavik and the capital area

Open all year: LaugardalurNo sleeping in vehicles in the city

The capital is where many trips start and end, and it is where many travellers are tempted to save money by sleeping on a quiet street. Guidance for Reykjavik is clear that sleeping in vehicles is not allowed within the city, and fines apply for unauthorised overnight stays in public areas. The simple solution is the Reykjavik campsite in Laugardalur, which one rental guide describes as open all year with showers, power hookups and laundry.

Paid parking zones also operate in the city centre, with charges on certain days and hours. The rules and fees change, so check the City of Reykjavik's parking pages for the current zones. If you are on the Reykjanes peninsula near the airport, one rental guide lists a campsite at Sandgerði among those that stay open through winter, but confirm that before you rely on it.

South Coast

Long camping seasonMarked areas only at some big sightsMany winter-open options

The South Coast carries most of Iceland's visitors, so there are plenty of campsites, and the season is one of the longest in the country. Skaftafell sits inside Vatnajökull National Park, where visitors are asked to use organised campsites. At Skógafoss, camping is only allowed in marked areas. Dyrhólaey and Ingólfshöfði are on the official list of places where camping and overnight stays are not allowed at all.

Þakgil is a popular choice, priced at about 3,000 ISK per person (age 12 and over) in one guide and open from June to the end of September, though the access road is rough and a 4x4 is recommended. Svínafell is listed at about 2,300 ISK per person with tent camping from May to September. One rental guide also lists Skaftafell, Skógafoss, Hveragerði (Reykjamörk) and Úthlíð in Biskupstungur among campsites that stay open in winter. Check each site's current dates before you plan around them.

West and Snæfellsnes

Campsites in townsMarked areas only near Arnarstapi and Hellnar

Snæfellsnes packs a lot of scenery into a small area, and the camping rules reflect that. Camping around Arnarstapi and Hellnar is allowed only in marked areas. In Snæfellsjökull National Park, hikers and cyclists carrying all their equipment may camp one night in designated areas, and anything longer or larger needs permission, which is another reason a campervan should use a proper campsite.

Hellissandur is listed at about 1,700 ISK per adult with reduced prices for some groups and an open season of May to September in one guide. Grundarfjörður is on a rental guide's list of campsites that stay open in winter. Akranes, Búðardalur and the Borgarnes area give you options on the way in from Reykjavik.

Westfjords

Small, simple campsitesSlow roadsCheck seasons and prices locally

The Westfjords are remote, beautiful and slow to drive, with winding roads that make a day feel twice as long as the map suggests. Campsites are small and simple. Examples include Drangsnes, Bolungarvík, Patreksfjörður and Tálknafjörður, and there are options near Ísafjörður and Dynjandi. The guides we read did not publish firm prices or seasons for this region, so call ahead or check the campsite's own page.

Dynjandi is one place to know about in advance: camping there is prohibited, although hikers and cyclists may camp for one night. Vatnsfjörður and Hornstrandir allow camping only in marked areas or with permission, and Hornstrandir is so remote that you must be fully self-sufficient. Plan shorter driving days, fill up on fuel when you can, and have a backup campsite in mind for every stop.

North Iceland: Akureyri and Mývatn

Ásbyrgi mid-May to OctoberNo overnight stays at several Mývatn sights

The north has good campsites and one important cluster of restrictions. Around Mývatn, camping is limited to marked areas, and overnight stays are not allowed at Dimmuborgir, Hverfjall (Hverfell) or Skútustaðagígar. Jökulsárgljúfur is also off limits for camping outside the proper sites. This is a classic place where travellers get caught out, because the landscape looks so open and quiet.

Ásbyrgi is priced at about 2,800 ISK per person in one guide, with children free when accompanying an adult, and an open season from mid-May to October. Husavik, Siglufjörður, Þórshöfn, Ólafsfjörður and Skagaströnd have campsites too. One rental guide lists Blönduós and Hamrar near Akureyri among the campsites open in winter.

East Iceland

Quietest regionHallormsstaðaskógur mid-May to September

The East is the calmest part of the Ring Road, and the pressure on campsites is lower than in the South or around Mývatn. Hallormsstaðaskógur, Iceland's largest forest, has campsites priced at roughly 2,000 to 2,200 ISK per night in one guide, open from mid-May to September, and Atlavík is a good choice for families. Seyðisfjörður and the Stuðlagil canyon area also have camping options.

Egilsstaðir and Djúpivogur are listed by one rental guide among campsites open in winter. Two places to avoid for overnight stays are Teigarhorn and Blábjörg on the Berufjörður coast, both on the official no-camping list.

The Highlands and F-roads

Late June to mid-SeptemberBasic facilitiesBook ahead

Highland campsites such as Landmannalaugar, Þórsmörk, Hrafntinnusker on the Laugavegur trail, Hólaskjól, Kerlingarfjöll and Básar usually run from late June to mid-September, with basic facilities. Many routes need a 4x4 on F-roads, or the seasonal Highland bus, and most standard rental campervans are not allowed on F-roads, so check your rental agreement before you plan a route inland.

In Fjallabak, overnight stays are allowed only in huts and designated camping grounds, and camping elsewhere needs a ranger's permission. Pre-booking is essential for Landmannalaugar in July and August. See the breakdown guide for why unsuitable roads are a bad place to test your van.

Places Where Overnight Stays Are Not Allowed

The Environment Agency of Iceland publishes a list of places where camping and overnight stays are not allowed. It is worth saving before you leave, because several of the places are among the most photographed in the country. At the time we checked, the list included:

  • Andakíll
  • Álafoss
  • Blábjörg (Berufjarðarströnd)
  • Bringur (Mosfellsdalur)
  • Dimmuborgir
  • Dyrhólaey
  • Hverfjall / Hverfell
  • Ingólfshöfði
  • Kattarauga
  • Kirkjugólf
  • Seljahjallagil, Bláhvammur, Þrengslaborgir and surrounding areas
  • Skútustaðagígar
  • Teigarhorn

Separately, camping is allowed only in marked areas or with permission at Arnarstapi and Hellnar, Hornstrandir, Mývatn, Skógafoss, Vatnsfjörður and Þingvellir National Park. Vatnajökull National Park asks visitors to use organised campsites for vehicles, and parts of it, including Jökulsárgljúfur and Skaftafellsheiði, are closed to camping entirely. Protected areas can also close temporarily during sensitive seasons, so look for signs and ask staff.

Rule of thumbIf a place is famous enough to have its own name on a postcard, assume you cannot sleep there unless there is a campsite with a name and a reception.

What a Night Costs, and Whether the Camping Card Pays Off

Budget travellers sometimes imagine that wild camping would save a lot of money. In a campervan, it will not, because it is not an option. The good news is that campsites are inexpensive compared with hotels and guesthouses in Iceland. Guides published for 2026 put most campsites at roughly 2,000 to 3,500 ISK per person per night. Showers and electricity often cost extra, and campsites inside national parks such as Ásbyrgi tend to charge more. One guide also reports a government accommodation tax of 400 ISK per night for each unit, and it is not covered by the Camping Card.

The Iceland Camping Card costs about 26,000 ISK (roughly 179 euros). It covers up to 28 nights for two adults and up to four children under 16 at more than 40 participating campsites, and it expires on 15 September each year. Some sites close earlier. It does not cover the tax, electricity or showers. Guides disagree a little on the break-even point, from about five nights to ten, because it depends on which campsites you choose. Fines and Enforcement

The Environment Agency's page does not list a fine amount, and the Icelandic Tourist Board's earlier notice on the topic describes the rule without one. Rental-company guides report fines starting at around 50,000 ISK, which was roughly 350 euros in the guide we read, and they say the rules are strictly enforced. Treat that as a rough guide rather than a fixed number, because penalties depend on the circumstances.

Two other points are worth remembering. Landowners and rangers can ask you to leave, and they can direct you to a nearby campsite. And driving off marked roads is a separate and more serious offence, so never use a field or track as a shortcut to a "private" spot. If you are unsure about a place, ask at the nearest campsite or tourist information office. They answer the question every day.

How to Plan Your Nights, Step by Step

Planning the nights is the part most people leave until the last minute, and it is also the part that causes most of the stress. These six steps keep it simple.

  1. Map your route and driving time. Decide roughly where you will be each evening. Iceland's roads are slower than they look, especially in the Westfjords and in bad weather.
  2. Pick a campsite near each stop. Choose one main option and one backup in case it is full or closed. Campsite directories such as Tjalda.is can help you compare facilities.
  3. Check dates and booking rules. Seasons vary by region and by site. Highland campsites open late and close early, and many town campsites stop taking guests in autumn.
  4. Book ahead in peak season. July and August are busy, and the early-August bank holiday weekend brings a nationwide spike in demand. Reykjavik-area sites fill up from June to August.
  5. Arrive in daylight. Finding a pitch is easier, and you will have time to ask the staff about tomorrow's weather and roads.
  6. Leave no trace. Use the toilets and waste facilities at the campsite and take everything with you. The rules exist to protect the places you came to see.

Summer Versus Winter: How the Season Changes Your Nights

Summer

Long daylight and plenty of open campsites make planning easy, but popular sites fill quickly, Highland campsites open only from late June, and booking ahead matters. Do not assume a pitch will be free at the end of a long day.

Winter

Many campsites close, and the ones that stay open are fewer and further apart. Daylight is short, roads can close at short notice and you will need good heating and gas. Check each campsite is open before you set out, and use road.is and SafeTravel.is for conditions.

Mistakes to Avoid

  • Assuming that "no sign" means "allowed".
  • Treating the tent rules as if they apply to a campervan.
  • Sleeping at a waterfall, crater or viewpoint car park because it looks quiet.
  • Arriving at a campsite late in July or August without a booking.
  • Driving a standard campervan onto an F-road to find a "secret" spot.
  • Relying on a campsite being open in autumn or winter without checking.
  • Leaving rubbish, or emptying waste anywhere except designated facilities.
  • Forgetting that the Camping Card does not cover the tax, showers or electricity.

Final Thoughts

The Iceland campervan overnight parking rules by region come down to one dependable habit: sleep at a campsite, and choose it before the day is over. Do that, and the rest of the trip gets easier. You will avoid fines, you will protect the places that make Iceland special and you will wake up with showers and a kettle nearby.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you sleep in a campervan anywhere in Iceland?

No. Under Iceland's Nature Conservation Act no. 60/2013, it is illegal to spend the night in camper vans, caravans and similar vehicles outside organised campsites or urban areas unless the landowner or rightholder gives permission. In practice, plan to sleep at a campsite every night.

Can I park my campervan overnight in a car park or lay-by?

Parking and sleeping are different things. Sleeping in the vehicle counts as camping, so roadsides, lay-bys and tourist car parks are not suitable places to spend the night. Some guides say you may leave a vehicle parked overnight wherever other cars may park, as long as you are not sleeping in it, but check signs and local fees.

What is the fine for sleeping in a campervan outside a campsite in Iceland?

The Environment Agency's page does not list an amount. Some rental-company guides report fines starting around 50,000 ISK and say the rules are strictly enforced, even where there is no sign. Treat that figure as a guide and not as official.

How much do campsites in Iceland cost for a campervan?

Guides published for 2026 put most campsites at roughly 2,000 to 3,500 ISK per person per night, with showers and electricity often extra and national park sites sometimes higher. One guide also reports a government accommodation tax of 400 ISK per unit per night. Always confirm prices with the campsite.

Is the Iceland Camping Card worth it?

It costs about 26,000 ISK (roughly 179 euros), covers up to 28 nights for two adults and up to four children under 16, and works at more than 40 participating campsites until 15 September. It does not cover the accommodation tax, electricity or showers. Guides put the break-even at roughly five to ten paid nights for a couple, so it suits longer trips best.

Can I camp in the Icelandic Highlands in a campervan?

Highland campsites such as Landmannalaugar, Þórsmörk and Kerlingarfjöll usually run from late June to mid-September with basic facilities. Many routes need a 4x4 on F-roads, and standard rental campervans are generally not allowed on them, so check your rental agreement and book ahead for July and August.
