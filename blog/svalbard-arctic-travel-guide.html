Svalbard: The Arctic Destination Most Travelers Overlook

August 15, 202611 min readWanderLust Blog

No roads connect its towns. Polar bears outnumber people. And it might be the strangest, most memorable place we've ever put on this list.

Svalbard sits roughly midway between mainland Norway and the North Pole, and it shows in almost everything about how the place operates — there's no road network connecting its settlements, no way to simply drive between towns the way you would almost anywhere else in Europe. Longyearbyen, the main settlement and the world's northernmost town of any real size, is where nearly every visit starts and ends.

Why the polar bear warning isn't a gimmick

Every visitor arriving in Longyearbyen sees the signs at the town limits warning about polar bears, and it's not tourist theater — Svalbard's bear population genuinely outnumbers its human residents, and stepping past the settlement boundary without an armed guide or your own firearm training is both discouraged and, in practice, rare among visitors who understand the risk. Every excursion company we found builds this into their trips as standard practice, not an optional upgrade.

The town itself

Longyearbyen is small — a single main street, a scattering of colorful wooden buildings originally built for the coal-mining industry that first settled the area, and a genuine sense that you've arrived somewhere fundamentally different from anywhere else on a normal travel itinerary. The town's supermarket, oddly enough, became one of our favorite stops — not for the shopping, but for the strange normalcy of a fully stocked grocery store this far into the Arctic.

Choosing a season

Midnight sun season, roughly late April through August, means the sun genuinely never sets, which reshapes sleep schedules in ways that take a few days to adjust to. Polar night, running roughly November through February, flips that entirely — full darkness around the clock, and considerably better odds of aurora sightings if the skies stay clear. We visited during the shoulder of midnight sun season and found the constant light disorienting in a way no amount of reading about it beforehand really prepared us for.

Boat trips and glaciers

Multi-day boat excursions around the archipelago's fjords are, by most accounts we gathered from other travelers and operators, the best way to actually see Svalbard's scale — glaciers calving into the sea, walrus colonies hauled out on rocky shores, a landscape that feels less like a destination and more like a different geological era entirely.

Book excursions well ahead if you're visiting in peak midnight-sun season — Longyearbyen's guided-trip capacity is limited, and demand has grown noticeably as remote, less-photographed destinations gain more attention.

The cost, honestly

Svalbard is not a budget destination, and it's worth going in with that expectation rather than being surprised by it. Flights carry a premium tied to the remoteness, guided excursions are priced accordingly given the specialized equipment and training involved, and even basic accommodation in Longyearbyen costs more than equivalent lodging almost anywhere else in Norway.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a passport to visit Svalbard?

Yes — even with its unique visa-free status under international treaty, a valid passport is required, and most travelers connect through mainland Norway, which does require standard Schengen-area documentation.

Is it actually dangerous because of polar bears?

Outside Longyearbyen's town limits, yes — an armed escort or licensed guide is required by local rule, since bear encounters, while rare, are taken seriously given the remoteness and lack of nearby help.

When can you see the midnight sun or northern lights?

The midnight sun runs roughly late April through August; polar night, with better aurora visibility, runs roughly November through February.

How expensive is a Svalbard trip?

Notably expensive — flights, guided excursions, and Longyearbyen accommodation all carry a premium tied to the logistics of operating this far north.
