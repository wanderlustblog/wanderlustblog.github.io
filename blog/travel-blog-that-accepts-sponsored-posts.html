Searching for a travel blog that accepts sponsored posts sounds easy until you start. Dozens of sites say they accept paid content. Some are real travel publications with engaged readers. Others are thin pages built only to sell links. Telling them apart before you pay is the part that saves money and protects your brand.

This guide is written for businesses, agencies and writers. It explains what "accepts sponsored posts" should mean, the checks to run before you buy, the red flags to avoid and how sponsored content works on WanderLust. If you already know you want to pay for a post, jump to our guide to a paid guest post on a travel blog for rates and process.

Plain-language noteSponsored content is advertising and should be labelled as such. We cannot promise any blog will deliver rankings, traffic or sales, ours included. This is general information, not legal advice, and rules differ by country.

What "Accepts Sponsored Posts" Should Mean

A good answer has four parts. The blog publishes paid content. It labels that content clearly as sponsored. It marks paid links appropriately. And it keeps the same editorial standards it applies to everything else. If a site does the first but not the other three, you are buying a link on a page, not a placement on a publication.

Real readers

The blog has an actual audience interested in travel, not just a catalogue of unrelated posts.

Clear labelling

Sponsored posts are marked so readers know they are advertising, which matches FTC guidance on native ads.

Proper link attributes

Paid links use rel="sponsored", which Google recommends for ads and paid placements.

Editorial standards

The blog edits and fact-checks paid content and may decline pieces that do not help readers.

Ten Checks Before You Pay Any Travel Blog

Spend twenty minutes on these checks. They cost nothing and filter out most poor choices.

  1. Read five recent posts. Are they useful, original and well edited, or thin and repetitive?
  2. Check the topics. Does your subject match what the blog covers, or would it look out of place?
  3. Look for a sponsored policy. A clear page or note about paid content is a good sign.
  4. Look for visible labels. Find an existing sponsored post and see how it is marked.
  5. Inspect the links. View the page source or use browser tools to see whether paid links carry rel="sponsored".
  6. Check the site on a phone. Pages should load, read well and work on mobile.
  7. Look at the contact details. A real email address and an about page signal a real person.
  8. Search the blog's name. See how it appears in search results and whether it looks like a genuine publication.
  9. Ask for the price in writing. Know what is included and what is not.
  10. Ask what they will not promise. An honest blog will say it cannot guarantee rankings or sales.

Red Flags That Should Make You Walk Away

  • Guaranteed rankings or traffic. Nobody controls search results.
  • No labelling. Hidden advertising may be deceptive. The FTC's native advertising guide says content that is not readily identifiable as an ad can mislead readers.
  • Hundreds of unrelated categories. A blog that accepts any topic usually has no real audience.
  • Pressure to stuff keywords. Repeated exact-match anchors look manipulative.
  • No visible author or contact details. You cannot hold an anonymous site accountable.
  • Very low prices for many links. Bulk packages often mean low quality.
A caution on linksGoogle's guidance says paid links should be qualified, and points to its spam policies for its position on paid links. It does not describe penalties on the page we read, so we make no claims about them. The safest course is to follow the guidance and label paid links.

Labelling: What Good Looks Like

The FTC's native advertising guide says wording such as "Ad", "Advertisement", "Paid Advertisement" or "Sponsored Advertising Content" is likely to be understood, while "Promoted" is ambiguous. It advises putting the disclosure immediately before or above the headline, keeping it large and readable, and not relying on a company logo alone. It also says there is no safe harbour and the guide dates from 2015, so treat it as a starting point and check the rules in your own country.

When you assess a blog, look for the same things: a plain label near the top, consistent wording and no attempt to blend an ad into editorial content.

Questions to Ask the Blog Owner

A short email before you pay reveals a lot. A good blog answers plainly. Here are questions worth sending.

  • What topics do you cover, and which of mine would suit your readers?
  • How do you label sponsored content, and where does the label appear?
  • Do paid links use rel="sponsored"?
  • Who writes and edits the post, and can I review it before it goes live?
  • What is the price, what does it include and is there any extra charge?
  • How long will the post stay live, and what happens if my page changes?
  • What do you not promise?

On WanderLust we are happy to answer these by email. We reply to every message, and we will tell you plainly if your topic is not a fit.

Common Mistakes Brands Make With Sponsored Posts

  • Writing a brochure. Readers skip pure promotion. Teach something first.
  • Choosing by price alone. A cheap post on a weak site is worth little.
  • Linking to the wrong page. Send readers to the page that matches the topic, not just your homepage.
  • Expecting instant results. Search visibility and enquiries take time and are never guaranteed.
  • Hiding the sponsorship. It damages trust and may breach advertising rules where you operate.
  • Ignoring mobile. Most travellers read on a phone, so check how the page looks and loads.

Measuring Results Honestly

Decide in advance what success means, and measure it with your own tools.

  • Referral visits. Use analytics to see visitors who arrive from the sponsored post.
  • Enquiries. Add a simple way to ask where visitors heard about you.
  • Brand visibility. Track mentions and searches for your name over time.
  • Patience. Search visibility can take time, and results vary. Do not judge a post in a week.

How do I know if a travel blog is worth sponsoring?

Read recent posts, check that topics match yours, look for visible sponsored labels, inspect link attributes and make sure the site works on mobile. Avoid anyone who guarantees rankings.

Should sponsored posts be labelled?

Yes. The FTC's native advertising guide says content that is not readily identifiable as an ad may be deceptive, and it lists wording such as "Ad" and "Advertisement" as likely to be understood.

Should paid links be nofollow or sponsored?

Google recommends rel="sponsored" for ads and paid placements and says nofollow is still acceptable. We use sponsored.

Can a sponsored post guarantee better Google rankings?

No. Nobody can guarantee rankings, traffic or sales. A sponsored post gives you a labelled article and link, and results vary.
