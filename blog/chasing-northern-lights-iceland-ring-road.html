Chasing the Northern Lights Across Iceland's Ring Road

April 22, 202614 min readby Maya Chen

Nine days, one campervan, and the most surreal skies on Earth. Every breakdown led to an unexpected discovery.

Back to Reykjavik

The Ring Road doesn't ease you in. Within an hour of leaving Reykjavik, the landscape turns lunar — black lava fields stretching to a horizon that never quite resolves into anything familiar. We'd rented a campervan with a heater that worked maybe sixty percent of the time, which in hindsight set the tone for the whole trip: nothing went quite to plan, and somehow that was the best part.

The south coast, fast

Most people rush the south coast to get to the ring proper, and it's a mistake worth avoiding if you can help it. Seljalandsfoss and Skogafoss get the crowds — busloads arriving on a tight schedule, cameras up before anyone's even parked — but Reynisfjara's black sand beach at low tide, with almost no one else around at 7am, is the shot everyone's chasing on Instagram and rarely actually gets. We stood there for the better part of an hour, just listening to the waves pull back over the volcanic sand, before another car showed up.

Vik itself is small enough to walk end to end in fifteen minutes, but it makes a good base for two nights if the weather turns, which in Iceland it does with almost comic frequency. We watched a clear morning become full sideways rain by lunch, then clear again by evening, all within the same six-hour stretch.

The east fjords, slower than planned

This is the stretch most itineraries shortchange, and we did too, on the way out at least. The road narrows and climbs along fjord walls with almost no traffic, small fishing villages appearing every twenty minutes or so, each one looking like it hasn't changed much in decades. We stopped for coffee in a village whose name we'd never heard before and haven't managed to find again on the map since — that's part of the charm of driving here without a rigid schedule.

The night the sky opened

We saw the lights on day six, parked on a gravel pull-off with no cell signal, no real plan, just a thermos of coffee and a sky that started as a faint green smear low on the horizon. Over the next twenty minutes it built into something none of us had words for — ribbons folding over each other, shifting from green to a pale violet at the edges, moving slowly enough that it felt less like weather and more like something being conducted. All four of us went completely silent, which for a van full of people who'd been bickering about playlist choices all week, said something.

North Iceland's quieter pace

Lake Mývatn and the geothermal area around it feel like a different country from the south coast — steam rising out of the ground in unexpected places, sulfur smell thick enough to notice from the road, and noticeably fewer other travelers than the Golden Circle crowds further south. Akureyri, the unofficial capital of the north, gave us the best meal of the whole trip, in a small restaurant that didn't take reservations and didn't need to.

What we'd do differently

  • Budget more time for the East Fjords — we rushed it on the way out and regretted it enough to slow down considerably by the return leg
  • Book campsite spots ahead in the Westfjords detour if you're adding it; they fill fast in summer and there's little cell signal to sort it out last minute
  • Bring real waterproofs, not "water-resistant" — the difference matters here more than almost anywhere else we've traveled
  • Don't skip the detour roads just because they add time; some of the best moments of the trip happened on routes that weren't on the original plan

Nine days wasn't enough. It rarely is, for a country that keeps rewriting its own weather every twenty minutes and somehow makes every single one of those rewrites worth pulling over for.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a 4x4 to drive the Ring Road?

No — the main Ring Road (Route 1) is paved and manageable in a standard car in summer. A 4x4 only matters for F-road highland detours.

Is the whole loop drivable in winter?

Yes, but sections can close temporarily after storms, and daylight hours are short. Summer is far easier for a first trip.

How likely am I to see the northern lights?

Best odds are September through March on clear, dark nights away from town lights — never guaranteed, but a multi-night stay improves your chances substantially.
