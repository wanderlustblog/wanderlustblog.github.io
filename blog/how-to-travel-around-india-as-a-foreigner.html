How to Travel Around India as a Foreigner: Complete Transport Guide

September 2, 202612 min readby WanderLust Blog

Figuring out how to travel around India as a foreigner is really a question of matching the right transport option to the right leg of your trip, rather than picking one method and sticking with it the whole way.

DistanceBest Option
Long jumps between regionsDomestic flight
City-to-city, scenicTrain (AC classes)
Within a congested cityMetro, then Uber/Ola
Short local hopsAuto-rickshaw (insist on meter)

A country this size runs on a genuine mix of trains, flights, metros, rickshaws, and ride-hailing apps, and most experienced travelers end up using several of them within a single week. This guide walks through each option honestly — what it's actually good for, what it costs relative to the others, and where the common mistakes and scams tend to show up. It pairs naturally with our broader guide on what foreign tourists need to know before visiting India if you're still in the earlier planning stages.

The Big Picture

Before the detail, here's the general shape of it: trains and flights handle the long distances between cities, metros and app-based rides handle getting around within a city, and auto-rickshaws and walking fill in the short, local gaps. Almost no first-time visitor uses just one of these — the trip works best when you treat them as complementary tools rather than competing choices.

Trains: The Classic Way Between Cities

India's rail network is enormous, and for tourists, it's often the most enjoyable way to cover ground between major destinations — comfortable in the air-conditioned classes, and a genuinely good way to see the countryside between stops. Our Golden Triangle itinerary guide covers the Delhi–Agra leg specifically, one of the most popular rail routes for first-timers.

How Foreigners Can Book Train Tickets

Foreign tourists (and NRIs) can book through IRCTC's official e-ticketing website, and there's a dedicated Foreign Tourist Quota (FTQ) worth knowing about specifically:

  • FTQ bookings can be made up to 365 days in advance — considerably further out than the standard advance booking window available to domestic passengers.
  • FTQ tickets are available in the higher classes only — First AC (1A), Second AC (2A), and Executive Chair Car (EC) — so it's not an option for budget-class travel.
  • You'll need to register on IRCTC and provide your passport details at the time of booking, and there's a service charge on top of the fare in addition to the ticket price — check IRCTC's website directly for the current amount, since fees are periodically updated.
  • You must carry your original passport and visa during the journey. Railway staff can check this, and travelers unable to produce it may be treated as ticketless and penalized.

If FTQ availability or the higher classes don't suit your budget or route, general quota booking (the same system domestic travelers use) is also open to foreign tourists, though with a shorter advance-booking window.

What to Expect Onboard

AC classes offer reserved berths or seats, on-board catering on many routes, and a level of comfort that surprises first-timers who've only heard train-travel horror stories from decades past. Sleeper and unreserved classes exist and are far cheaper, but are a considerably more intense experience — worth trying once for the experience, less practical as your default for every journey.

Domestic Flights: Best for Long Jumps

For genuinely long distances — Delhi to Kerala, for instance, or anywhere a train would eat most of a day — domestic flights make the most practical sense. India has a competitive domestic airline market with frequent routes between major cities, and for a two-week itinerary spanning distant regions, at least one flight leg is common even among budget-conscious travelers.

Buses: Flexible, Uneven in Quality

Long-distance buses connect virtually every town and city in India, including many places trains don't reach directly. Quality varies significantly — private "Volvo" AC coaches on popular tourist routes tend to be comfortable and reliable, while older state-run buses on less-traveled routes can be considerably rougher. Overnight buses are common for medium-distance routes and can save a hotel night, though light sleepers may prefer a train berth instead.

Metro Systems: Fast, Cheap, and Underused by Tourists

Several major Indian cities — Delhi, Mumbai, Bengaluru, Chennai, Hyderabad, and Kolkata among them — have modern metro systems that are clean, air-conditioned, and dramatically faster than surface traffic during peak hours. Tourists sometimes overlook metros in favor of taxis out of unfamiliarity, which is a missed opportunity: metro travel is often the fastest way to cross a congested city like Delhi at rush hour, and stations are typically well-signed in English alongside the local language.

Ride-Hailing Apps: Uber and Ola

Uber operates in most major Indian cities, and Ola, India's homegrown equivalent, is equally widespread — many travelers keep both installed, since pricing and driver availability can vary between the two block by block. Both offer:

  • Upfront, metered pricing rather than negotiation
  • In-app driver and vehicle details for accountability
  • A straightforward alternative to hailing an unmarked vehicle on the street

For predictable, hassle-free short trips within a city, ride-hailing apps are generally the least stressful option for first-time visitors — our dedicated guide on using Uber in India as a foreign tourist covers account setup and payment specifics in more depth.

Auto-Rickshaws: Cheap and Fast for Short Hops

Auto-rickshaws remain one of the fastest ways to cover short distances in dense urban traffic, and they're genuinely useful once you know how to use them without overpaying:

  • Insist on the meter where one exists, or agree on a price clearly before getting in if it doesn't.
  • If a driver claims the meter is "broken," treat that as a signal to either negotiate firmly or find another rickshaw.
  • Many cities now have app-based auto-rickshaw booking through Uber or Ola, which removes the negotiation step entirely.

Taxis and Prepaid Airport Counters

Traditional taxis remain common, particularly at airports, where many terminals operate prepaid taxi counters — you pay a fixed fare at the counter before getting in, which removes any ambiguity about the cost of the ride into the city. This is generally a safer and simpler option for a first arrival than negotiating with drivers directly outside the terminal.

Walking: Underrated in the Right Areas

Walking is a genuinely good way to experience specific, walkable pockets of Indian cities — old quarters, market districts, riverfronts — where the density of things to see rewards a slower pace better than any vehicle would. That said, general city-to-city or even neighborhood-to-neighborhood walking is less practical given traffic patterns, heat for much of the year, and the sheer scale of most Indian cities, so it's best treated as a tool for specific areas rather than a primary mode of transport. Whatever the day involves, dressing for it matters too — our guide on what to wear in India as a tourist covers footwear and fabric choices that hold up to a long walking day.

Staying Safe and Avoiding Transport Scams

A few habits address most of the transport-related friction foreign tourists run into:

  • Use metered or app-based transport wherever possible, rather than negotiating cold with an unfamiliar driver.
  • Agree on a price before you get in if no meter is available, and treat "the meter is broken" as a cue to double-check rather than accept at face value.
  • Use official prepaid taxi counters at airports rather than accepting offers from touts in the arrivals area.
  • Book train and bus tickets through official channels — IRCTC directly for trains, and reputable, established operators for long-distance buses — rather than through unverified third-party sellers.
  • Keep your passport and visa accessible but secure while traveling by train, since FTQ tickets specifically require them for verification.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can foreigners book Indian train tickets online?

Yes, through IRCTC's official e-ticketing website, including a dedicated Foreign Tourist Quota with its own advance-booking window and available classes.

What is the Foreign Tourist Quota (FTQ) for Indian trains?

A booking category specifically for foreign tourists and NRIs, allowing reservations up to 365 days in advance in First AC, Second AC, or Executive Chair Car classes.

Does Uber work in India?

Yes, Uber operates in most major Indian cities, alongside the equally widespread local alternative, Ola.

Is it safe to use auto-rickshaws in India?

Generally yes, especially if you insist on the meter or agree a price beforehand — the main risk is overpaying, not personal safety.

What documents do I need to carry when taking a train in India?

If you've booked under the Foreign Tourist Quota, you're required to carry your original passport and visa for verification during the journey.

Are Indian metro systems easy for tourists to use?

Yes — major city metros are modern, air-conditioned, and typically signed in English alongside the local language, making them genuinely tourist-friendly.

Should I take a domestic flight or a train for long distances in India?

Flights make more sense for very long distances where a train would consume most of a day; trains are often preferable for shorter, scenic routes between nearby cities.

How do I avoid taxi overcharging at Indian airports?

Use the airport's official prepaid taxi counter, where available, rather than negotiating with drivers or touts in the arrivals hall.

Is it normal to negotiate prices with rickshaw drivers in India?

Only when there's no working meter — in that case, agree on a price before getting in rather than negotiating after the ride.

Can I walk around Indian cities safely as a tourist?

Yes, in specific walkable districts, though most Indian cities are large enough that walking works better as a supplement to other transport than as your primary way of getting around.
