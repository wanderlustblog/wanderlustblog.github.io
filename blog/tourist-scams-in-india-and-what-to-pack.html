Tourist Scams in India to Avoid & What to Pack

September 2, 20269 min readby WanderLust Blog

A handful of scam patterns come up often enough across India's major tourist circuits — Delhi, Agra, Jaipur, Varanasi — that they're worth knowing before you land, not after you've already been caught out.

None of these patterns are unique to India — most tourist-heavy countries have their own versions — but knowing the specific local flavor of each one in advance means you'll recognize it the moment it starts rather than partway through.

The Most Common Scam Patterns

1. The "Your Hotel Is Closed" Scam

A stranger, often near a train station, insists your booked hotel is closed or overbooked, and offers to take you somewhere else — which pays them a commission. A booking already confirmed online is reliable; a stranger's claim that it's suddenly not available isn't. Politely decline and head straight to your actual hotel to confirm in person.

2. Unmetered Taxi and Auto-Rickshaw Overcharging

Drivers claiming the meter is "broken," or quoting inflated flat fares to visibly unfamiliar tourists, are common enough near major stations and monuments to expect it. Insist on the meter, or agree on a price clearly before getting in if it's unmetered — app-based ride-hailing sidesteps this entirely.

3. Fake "Government Tourist Offices"

Storefronts, often near major stations, styled to look official but unaffiliated with any real government tourism body. They typically steer visitors toward overpriced tours, transport, or accommodation. Genuine tourism offices are listed on official government sites — worth confirming before trusting a storefront's signage alone.

4. Unlicensed Guides at Monuments

Self-appointed guides offering tours outside major sites, sometimes with inaccurate information and undisclosed, inflated fees revealed only at the end. Booking a guide through your hotel or the site's official booking desk avoids this entirely, and usually costs about the same.

5. Gem, Carpet, and Textile "Investment" Pitches

Friendly, often lengthy conversations that lead to a shop selling overpriced or misrepresented goods, framed as a good resale opportunity back home. If a casual chat starts steering toward a "special price, just for you" shop visit, that's the pattern showing itself.

How to Avoid Them

  • Book accommodation and transport in advance through verified platforms, and don't discuss your itinerary with strangers who approach you unprompted.
  • Use metered or app-based transport, and confirm the price before you get in if there's no meter.
  • Buy monument tickets only at official counters or online, and book guides through your hotel or the site's official booking desk.
  • Treat unsolicited offers of help near stations and major monuments with a healthy dose of skepticism — genuine helpfulness in India is common, but so is this specific setup.

For a broader look at general safety habits beyond scams specifically, our guide on solo female travel safety in India covers the same alert-but-not-anxious mindset that serves well here too. And if something does go beyond a scam into a genuine emergency, India's national emergency number, 112, connects to police, fire, and ambulance — details on how it works are on the official Emergency Response Support System site.

What to Pack

A first-timer's packing list doesn't need to be exotic — it needs to match the actual conditions you'll encounter:

  • Lightweight, breathable clothing, plus a few modest options for temples and religious sites
  • A warmer layer if traveling to North India between December and February
  • Comfortable, slip-on-friendly shoes
  • A basic first-aid kit and any personal medication in original packaging
  • Sunscreen and insect repellent
  • A universal power adapter
  • Hand sanitizer and tissues (not always provided in public restrooms)
  • Copies of your passport, visa, and travel insurance details, stored separately from the originals

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I avoid taxi and auto-rickshaw scams in India?

Use metered transport or app-based ride-hailing like Uber or Ola, and agree on a price up front if there's no meter.

Is it normal for strangers to approach and offer help in India?

Genuine helpfulness is common, but so is a specific scam pattern built around unsolicited help near stations and monuments — be polite but cautious, especially regarding hotel or booking claims.

What should I pack for a first trip to India?

Lightweight breathable clothing plus a few modest options for temples, a warmer layer if visiting North India in winter, comfortable slip-on shoes, a basic first-aid kit, sunscreen, insect repellent, a universal power adapter, and copies of your travel documents stored separately from the originals.

What is the hotel-is-closed scam in India?

A stranger, often near a train station, insists your booked hotel is closed or overbooked and offers to take you somewhere else that pays them a commission — a booking already confirmed online is reliable, so treat this claim with skepticism.
