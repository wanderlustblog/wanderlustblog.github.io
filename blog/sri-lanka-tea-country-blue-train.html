Sri Lanka's Tea Country: Riding the Blue Train Through the Hills

December 15, 20259 min readby Priya Nair

The most scenic train journey in Asia winds through emerald plantations and mist-covered peaks — and costs less than a coffee.

The Kandy to Ella line is routinely called one of the world's most beautiful train journeys, and having ridden it with the door open and legs hanging out over tea plantations for six-plus hours, it's genuinely hard to disagree with that reputation, overused as it might be in guidebook marketing copy.

Booking the right seat

Reserve second-class seats in advance specifically for the Kandy–Ella leg — unreserved third class gets packed standing-room only during peak season, and we watched more than a few visitors miss the best views entirely while fighting simply to find a spot near a window. We booked ours about two weeks ahead through a local agent and had assigned seats on the side of the carriage facing the best plantation views for most of the journey.

The journey itself

Leaving Kandy, the train climbs gradually through increasingly dramatic terrain — dense jungle giving way to terraced tea plantations, the track occasionally hugging cliff edges with views down into valleys that seem to drop away entirely. Vendors move through the carriages selling tea, snacks, and fruit throughout the journey, turning what could be a simple transit into something closer to a slow-moving picnic with scenery attached.

Stops worth making

Nuwara Eliya's cool hill-station air — a legacy of British colonial-era tea planters who built the town explicitly to escape the coastal heat — and Haputale's cliffside plantations both deserve an overnight stop rather than just a window-seat glance as the train passes through. We broke our journey with two nights in Nuwara Eliya, walking through working tea estates in the surrounding hills and touring a small factory where we watched the entire process from leaf to packaged tea in under an hour.

Nine Arch Bridge

Just outside Ella, the Nine Arch Bridge has become something of a photography pilgrimage site, with a small viewing area that fills up considerably around the times trains are scheduled to cross. We went at both a scheduled crossing time, for the classic shot, and again at a quiet hour with no train due, simply to appreciate the structure and the jungle around it without the crowd.

Ella itself

The town of Ella has grown considerably as a backpacker hub in recent years, and it shows in the density of cafes and guesthouses along its main street, but the hikes just outside town — up to Little Adam's Peak or the longer climb to Ella Rock — still offer genuine quiet and some of the best panoramic views of the surrounding hill country.

What to pack for the ride

A light layer for the cooler hill-country air, sunscreen for the hours spent leaning out an open door, and patience for a journey that runs on its own unhurried schedule rather than a strict timetable. Delays of thirty minutes to an hour aren't unusual, and treating the trip itself as the destination, rather than a means of simply getting to Ella, makes the inevitable slow patches far more enjoyable.

At a genuinely low cost for a journey through some of Asia's most striking scenery, it's hard to find better value for the experience anywhere else on the continent.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to book train tickets ahead?

Reserved second-class seats are worth booking in advance, especially in peak season, since unreserved cars get very crowded.

Is the journey worth doing in one sitting?

Many travelers prefer breaking it up with an overnight stop in Nuwara Eliya or Haputale rather than riding straight through.

What's the best seat for views?

A window seat on the left side heading from Kandy to Ella generally faces the most dramatic tea-plantation views.
