Hogmanay in Edinburgh 2026-27: Tickets, Events and How to Plan New Year in Scotland

October 23, 202611 min readby WanderLust Blog

Edinburgh's Hogmanay is one of the world's best-known New Year celebrations, and it works best when you plan tickets, accommodation and clothing well before you arrive.

Hogmanay is the Scottish word for the last day of the year and the celebrations that go with it. Edinburgh turns the festival into a multi-day event with a major street party, concerts, fireworks over the castle and a long tradition of first-footing, the custom of being the first person to cross a threshold in the new year. This guide explains how the celebration works, how to approach tickets, where to stay and how to dress, so you can enjoy it without the typical mistakes.

How Edinburgh's Hogmanay Is Structured

The official programme usually runs for several days around the end of the year, often from about December 29 to January 1, with details changing each season. In recent years it has typically included a torchlight procession through the Old Town, a large street party in the city centre with live music, a midnight fireworks display around Edinburgh Castle, and a range of concerts and events across the city. Many of the main events are ticketed with limited capacity, and access points are controlled, so check the official Edinburgh's Hogmanay website for the 2026-27 programme, ticket types, ages and entry rules. Do not rely on last year's details.

Tickets: What to Know Before You Buy

  • Buy only from the official website or authorised sellers, since resale scams are common for popular events.
  • Check ticket types carefully, such as general entry, premium areas and concerts, and read what each includes.
  • Check age rules and the policy on bags and alcohol, since security checks are strict.
  • Keep digital tickets on your phone, with a screenshot in case of poor signal, and carry photo ID.
  • If tickets are sold out, look for free viewpoints, ticketed parties in hotels and venues, or smaller community events.

Scottish Traditions You Will Hear About

First-footing is the tradition of being the first guest to enter a home after midnight, ideally bringing small gifts such as coal, shortbread, whisky or black bun for good luck. Auld Lang Syne, the song attributed to the poet Robert Burns, is sung at midnight with crossed hands. The Loony Dook is a New Year's Day swim in the cold sea at South Queensferry, west of the city, usually in fancy dress. These customs vary by family and region, but they give the celebration a clear character that sets it apart from other New Year events.

A 4-Day Edinburgh Plan Around Hogmanay

Day 1: Arrive and walk the Old Town

Check in, walk the Royal Mile, and visit a museum or café while it is still light. If the torchlight procession is on, take a look from a safe viewpoint. Eat early, since restaurants fill up for festival nights.

Day 2: Castle, galleries and rest

Visit Edinburgh Castle in the morning, prebooking tickets if needed. Rest in the afternoon before an evening concert, show or pub session.

Day 3: Hogmanay night

Keep the day light. Eat a proper meal, dress warmly and plan how you will get back to your accommodation after midnight, when crowds and road closures slow movement.

Day 4: New Year's Day

Sleep in, take a slow walk up Calton Hill or Arthur's Seat if the weather is good, and watch the Loony Dook if you want to see Scottish eccentricity at its best. Many shops and attractions open late or on reduced hours on January 1, so check before you go.

Where to Stay and How Early to Book

Hotels near the city centre and the Royal Mile sell out well before the festival and prices rise sharply. Book as early as possible, ideally with free cancellation. Staying slightly outside the centre, near a tram, bus or rail link, can be cheaper and calmer, but check transport hours and road closures on the main night, since the city centre becomes pedestrian-only in places. Many guests choose apartments or guesthouses for groups, but check cancellation terms and minimum stay requirements on New Year's Eve.

Getting to Edinburgh and Around the City

Edinburgh has an international airport with a tram line to the city centre, and trains from London and Glasgow arrive at Waverley Station in the heart of the city. Book rail tickets early, since New Year services are busy. In the city, walking is the best way to get around the Old Town and New Town, though hills and cobbles are tiring. On Hogmanay night, public transport may run differently and taxis and ride-hailing services are in high demand, so plan your route home before you go out and keep a charged phone with offline maps; see our offline maps guide.

What to Wear: Scottish Winter Reality

Edinburgh in late December is cold, damp and windy, and you will spend hours outdoors. Wear thermal layers, a warm mid-layer, a waterproof and windproof coat, waterproof boots with good grip, warm socks, gloves, a hat and a scarf. Avoid thin shoes, umbrellas that will not survive the wind and bags that are too large for security checks. Have a dry change of clothes at your accommodation. Our winter packing list has a good checklist that works for Edinburgh as well.

Food, Drink and Local Customs

Try Scottish dishes such as haggis with neeps and tatties, smoked salmon, cullen skink soup and shortbread. Whisky is a local speciality, and many pubs offer tasting flights. Drink responsibly, since the weather and crowds add risk. Scotland has specific rules on public drinking, and event security may confiscate alcohol, so check conditions of entry. Tipping is appreciated but not mandatory, and many restaurants add a service charge to larger groups.

Safety and Crowd Management

  • Agree a meeting point with your group, since mobile networks can become overloaded.
  • Keep valuables in inner pockets, and be aware of pickpockets in crowds.
  • Follow stewards' instructions, particularly at entry points and exit routes.
  • Do not drink and walk near steep stairs, closes (narrow lanes) or roads in icy conditions.
  • Keep emergency contacts and your accommodation address saved offline.

Alternatives If You Miss the Big Events

Edinburgh offers plenty of smaller options: pub sessions with live folk music, ceilidhs where you can learn Scottish country dancing, restaurant dinners, and viewpoints that give a distant look at the fireworks. Cities such as Glasgow, Inverness and Stonehaven also host their own New Year events. Stonehaven's fireball festival, in particular, is a distinctive local tradition. Check local programmes and book accommodation early in any of these places.

Budget Planning Without Guesswork

New Year's Eve in Edinburgh is expensive, and prices change each year. Build your own budget by listing travel, accommodation for at least three nights, event tickets, food and drink, local transport, clothing you need to buy and a buffer of 10 to 15 percent. Compare three or four accommodation options for the exact dates, and check whether breakfast, taxes and cancellation are included. Consider arriving earlier or leaving later if prices are lower on the shoulder days.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Leaving hotels and tickets to the last minute.
  • Underestimating the cold and damp, and wearing fashion shoes.
  • Not planning how to get back to your hotel after midnight.
  • Buying tickets from unofficial resellers.
  • Assuming everything will be open on January 1.

Final Checklist

  • Official programme checked and tickets booked from authorised sellers.
  • Hotel and trains booked with cancellation terms checked.
  • Waterproof clothing, boots, power bank and offline maps ready.
  • Plan for midnight, meeting points and the journey home.

A Scottish tradition in which the first person to cross a threshold after midnight brings good luck, often with small gifts such as shortbread or coal.
