The Inca Trail: What Nobody Tells You Before You Go

March 8, 202613 min readby Maya Chen

Altitude, relentless rain, blisters — and then the absolute, tearful awe of Machu Picchu materialising from the morning mist.

Machu Picchu

Nobody fully prepares you for Dead Woman's Pass on the way to Machu Picchu. At just over four thousand meters, every step above roughly four thousand feels like moving through wet concrete, and most guidebooks undersell exactly how much that altitude reshapes the entire second day of the trek.

What actually helps

  • Spend at least two full days in Cusco acclimatizing before starting — we did one and regretted the shortcut on day two
  • Coca tea genuinely helps more than most Western altitude remedies we tried, even if the mechanism is more folk wisdom than clinical study
  • Pack layers you can strip fast — temperature swings between cloud forest and exposed ridgeline are brutal and fast
  • Hire a porter if your budget allows it; carrying a full pack over the pass adds a level of difficulty that isn't necessary to prove anything

The trail itself, day by day

Day one eases you in — gentle climbs, Inca ruins scattered along the valley, a false sense that this might all be manageable. Day two disabuses you of that notion entirely with the climb to Dead Woman's Pass, roughly seven hours of steady, grinding ascent that tests legs and lungs in roughly equal measure. Day three drops back down through cloud forest, genuinely one of the most beautiful stretches of trail either of us has walked anywhere, thick with orchids and the sound of unseen birds. Day four starts before dawn for the final approach to the Sun Gate.

The Sun Gate moment

Nothing quite prepares you for reaching Inti Punku at sunrise on day four and watching Machu Picchu resolve slowly out of the mist in the valley below. Four days of blisters, altitude headaches, and a porter's polite but firm insistence that we were walking too slowly all seemed to evaporate in about thirty seconds. We sat there for nearly twenty minutes before continuing down, not saying much, which for our group was unusual.

Inside the citadel

Machu Picchu itself, once you're actually inside the site rather than viewing it from the Sun Gate, rewards slower exploration than the standard guided tour allows. The agricultural terraces on the site's edges see far fewer visitors than the central temple structures, and offer a quieter vantage point looking back at the whole complex once the mid-morning crowds arrive by train and bus.

Food on the trail

Porters and cooks on the classic route produce meals that genuinely exceeded our expectations for a multi-day trek — soup, fresh vegetables, and even the occasional baked treat appeared at camp each evening, carried in on someone else's back. It's worth tipping generously; the crews carrying tents, food, and gear over the same pass that leaves hikers exhausted are doing considerably more physical work than the paying trekkers.

Alternatives if permits sell out

The classic Inca Trail's daily permits are limited and often sell out months ahead, especially for June through August departures. The Salkantay trek, a longer and more remote alternative route, doesn't require the same permit system and offers a comparable sense of accomplishment, arriving at Machu Picchu by a different but equally dramatic approach.

Book six months out if the classic trail is what you want — permits are capped and this is not a trek where you can improvise a booking a few weeks before arrival.

Frequently Asked Questions

How far ahead do I need to book the Inca Trail?

Permits are limited and commonly sell out months in advance, especially for June–August departures — book as early as you can.

Do I need trekking experience?

Some hiking fitness helps, but the trail is walked by first-timers regularly; the altitude is the bigger challenge, not technical difficulty.

Is altitude sickness a real risk?

Yes — spending a couple of days acclimatizing in Cusco before starting significantly reduces the risk.
