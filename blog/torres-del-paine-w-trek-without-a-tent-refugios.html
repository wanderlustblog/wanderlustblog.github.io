Torres del Paine W Trek Without a Tent: Refugios, Costs and Rules

November 27, 20269 min readby WanderLust Blog

The W trek in Torres del Paine is one of the great walks of South America, and you do not need a tent, a stove or a tent-pitching routine to do it. The park has refugios, which are mountain lodges with bunks, meals and showers, run by two companies. Sleeping in them keeps your pack light and your evenings warm in a place where the wind has a reputation.

This guide explains how a tent-free W trek works: the route and stages, which company runs which refugio, what the beds really cost, what to bring and the rules that catch first-timers out. When you are ready to book, our companion guide on Patagonia refugio booking tips for first-timers covers the booking steps in detail, and our Everest Base Camp beginner guide shows how another lodge-to-lodge trek compares.

Confirm everything before you bookPrices, rules and operators change every season. This guide draws on a W trek planning page checked on 8 October 2026, a booking article checked on 13 October 2026, a hut-to-hut tour operator's page and an older campsite guide for 2024/25. The sources disagree in places, so we flag where. Check the park authority CONAF and the two refugio companies before you pay for anything.

The W Trek in Four Stages

The W gets its name from the shape of the route on a map. Most people walk east to west, and the full classic route is about 76 km from Hotel Las Torres to Paine Grande with all three side trips, taking four to five days, according to a booking guide. Ending at Grey and leaving by boat is about 65 km. A tour operator's page gives the four stages below.

StageRouteDistanceOvernight
1Welcome Center to the Base of the Towers and backAbout 19 km returnRefugio Torre Central or Chileno (Las Torres)
2Torre Central to Los Cuernos11.6 kmRefugio Los Cuernos (Las Torres)
3Los Cuernos to Paine Grande via the French Valley and Mirador BritánicoAbout 23 km including the viewpointRefugio Paine Grande (Vertice)
4Paine Grande to Grey11 kmRefugio Grey (Vertice)

The operator rates the walk medium overall, with only the last 1.4 km below the towers and the 5.4 km climb to Mirador Británico described as demanding. It says you need fitness, not climbing skill.

Who Runs the Refugios

Two companies run the lodges, and you book each separately. This matters because your nights must line up.

Las Torres Patagonia

Runs the eastern sectors. A booking guide lists Central, Chileno, Cuernos and Francés, while another page lists Central, Chileno (camping only), Francés, Cuernos and Serón. The sources disagree about Chileno, so confirm what is offered.

Vertice Travel

Runs the western sectors: Paine Grande and Grey, plus Dickson and Los Perros on the O circuit. Refugio beds at Paine Grande and Grey are the standard tent-free stops.

CONAF

The park authority. It requires reservations for every night and checks them at ranger stations. Its free campsites were closed in 2025-26, with 2026-27 status unpublished as of 8 October 2026.

Tour operators

Some sell packages that book beds, buses, boat and entry for you. One self-guided 7-day tour starts from about 2,030 euros per person according to its own page.

Season, Daylight and Weather

The season runs from October to April, with gates open from 7 am to 9 pm. December to February has the longest days and the fullest camps, and a tour operator says daylight is about 16.5 hours at the December solstice. October, November, March and April are quieter and colder, and some camps close early: in 2025-26, Cuernos and Serón closed on 30 March, and Central and Chileno on 25 April. The westerly winds are strongest from November to January, according to the operator.

Trails close at set times, and walking without daylight is prohibited. One source says CONAF currently requires microspikes and trekking poles where winter conditions persist, so ask when you book.

What a Refugio Day Looks Like

The rhythm is simple. You wake in a shared dorm, eat breakfast at the refugio, walk with a day pack and arrive at the next lodge in the afternoon. Stage 3 is the longest, at about 23 km and around 11 hours including the French Valley viewpoint, so the operator's page suggests it is the day to start early. Stage 1 is a long out-and-back to the base of the towers, around 19 km return.

  • Morning: check the wind and the forecast with the refugio staff before you commit to a viewpoint.
  • Midday: carry lunch or pay for a packed lunch where the refugio offers one. Our sources do not give lunch prices.
  • Afternoon: arrive in daylight, since walking without daylight is prohibited.
  • Evening: dinner is served at set times at full-board refugios, and your reservation is checked on arrival.

Keep your confirmation, passport and park entry QR code together in a waterproof pouch.

What to Pack Without a Tent

The operator's packing advice is sensible for a refugio trip.

  • Clothing: waterproof jacket and trousers, a warm mid-layer, a down jacket, a warm hat and gloves even in summer, and sun protection.
  • Gear: broken-in boots, a head torch, a small first-aid kit, a power bank and trekking poles.
  • Sleeping: a sleeping bag or liner, and a padlock.
  • Cash: pesos for the Laguna Amarga shuttle, listed at CLP 4,500 each way in one guide.
  • Seasonal: microspikes in spring and autumn, since CONAF may turn walkers back from the towers without them.

Check which gear you can hire. One 2024/25 guide lists sleeping bags at USD 28 to 35 per day at the campsites and cheaper hire in Puerto Natales, but that guide covers campsites, not necessarily refugio beds.

Rules That Catch First-Timers Out

  • Reservations are mandatory for every night, and rangers check them.
  • Park entry is online only at pasesparques.cl, at least 24 hours ahead, shown as a QR code.
  • No wild camping, no walking without daylight and no fires outside designated cooking areas.
  • Stove rules. Stoves are allowed only in cooking shelters at some camps, and one source says not at Chileno.
  • Register at every ranger station.
  • Guides. Not required in summer, but required in winter with a ratio of 1 guide per 4 trekkers on the dates given for 2026.

Final Thoughts

A tent-free W trek is a comfortable way to see Torres del Paine, as long as you book early, match your nights across two companies and pack for wind and cold. Expect the beds to cost real money, especially on the Las Torres side, and confirm each price and rule yourself. Then all you have to do is walk.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I do the W trek without a tent?

Yes. Refugios run by Las Torres Patagonia and Vertice offer beds and meals along the route, so you can walk lodge to lodge without a tent or stove. Reservations are mandatory for every night.

Who runs the refugios on the W trek?

Las Torres Patagonia runs the eastern sectors, and Vertice Travel runs Paine Grande and Grey in the west. You book each company separately.

How much does a refugio bed cost on the W?

Sources list Las Torres beds from about USD 200 to 230 per night, bed only, and Vertice beds at about USD 46 to 66 for a bare bunk. Prices change, so confirm with each company.

Do refugio beds include a sleeping bag?

Not clearly. A tour operator says Vertice beds come with only a mattress cover and pillow, so bring a sleeping bag or liner. Our sources do not say what Las Torres dorms include.

When is the W trek season?

October to April, with gates open from 7 am to 9 pm. December to February is busiest, and shoulder months are quieter and colder with some camps closing early.

Do I need a guide for the W trek?

Not in summer. In winter, an accredited guide is required, and the W was fully closed in June and July 2026, according to one page.
