Living Root Bridges of Meghalaya: A First-Timer's Guide

August 26, 202610 min readby WanderLust Blog

Grown by hand over generations rather than built, Meghalaya's living root bridges are unlike anything else we've stood on anywhere in the world — and getting to one is half the story.

Quick TakeDetails
Base townCherrapunji (Sohra) or Tyrna village
Main trekNongriat double-decker bridge, ~3,500 steps round trip
Best seasonOctober–April for drier trails
Time neededFull day for Nongriat; 3-4 days to see multiple bridges

The Khasi and Jaintia communities of Meghalaya have been training the roots of rubber fig trees across rivers for well over a century, guiding growth along bamboo scaffolding until the roots themselves become strong enough to walk on. The result is a bridge that gets stronger with age rather than weaker — some of the oldest are estimated at well over 100 years old and still in daily use by villagers.

Getting to the trailhead

Most first-time visitors base themselves in Cherrapunji, one of the wettest places on Earth, before heading to Tyrna village, the actual starting point for the most famous trek. From Tyrna, the path down to Nongriat drops steeply through rainforest via roughly 3,500 stone and iron steps — genuinely steep enough that the climb back up is the part people remember most vividly, not the descent.

The double-decker bridge at Nongriat

The main draw is a genuinely rare structure: two living root bridges stacked one above the other, spanning the same river at different heights. Reaching it typically takes two to three hours down from Tyrna, with enough stream crossings and suspension bridges along the way that the trek itself feels like an attraction, not just a means of getting somewhere.

Quieter bridges beyond Nongriat

Nongriat gets the attention, but Meghalaya has dozens of living root bridges scattered across its river valleys, many reachable only with a local guide and a good pair of legs. Villages further from the main trail — Mawlynnong and Rangthylliang among them — offer a genuinely quieter version of the same experience, closer in spirit to what we found seeking out hidden waterfalls off Bali's main circuit: fewer crowds, more effort, and a stronger sense of having found something rather than been shown it.

What the trek is actually like

Be honest with yourself about fitness before committing to Nongriat in a single day — the descent is manageable for most reasonably active travelers, but the climb back up, often in humid heat, is the genuine physical test. We'd compare the effort level to a full day on Hallstatt's more demanding Alpine trails, minus the altitude but plus the humidity.

Monsoon versus dry season

The monsoon months turn Meghalaya's waterfalls dramatic and its forests impossibly green, but they also turn the stone steps slippery and bring out leeches on the lower stretches of trail. We'd point first-timers toward the October to April window, when the trails are drier and considerably more manageable, saving the monsoon spectacle for a second visit once you know what you're walking into.

Planning around official guidance

Trail conditions, permit requirements for certain routes, and seasonal closures can shift, so it's worth checking current information before finalizing dates rather than relying solely on trip reports. The Meghalaya Tourism Department, the state's official tourism body, is the most reliable source for up-to-date trail and travel advisories.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take to reach the living root bridges?

The trek to the famous double-decker bridge near Nongriat involves roughly 3,500 steps down and back, typically taking 3-4 hours round trip at a comfortable pace.

What is the best time of year to visit the root bridges?

October to April offers drier trails and clearer views; the monsoon months bring dramatic waterfalls but slippery steps and leech-heavy paths.

Do I need a guide to see the living root bridges?

Not strictly required for the main Nongriat trail, but a local guide adds context on how the bridges are grown and trained, and helps on lesser-marked routes to quieter bridges.
