Iceland Campervan Breakdown: What to Do and Who to Call (Ring Road Guide)

November 23, 202613 min readby WanderLust Blog

A campervan breakdown in Iceland feels very different from one at home. The next fuel station can be an hour away, your phone signal may vanish, and the weather can change faster than you can find your gloves. The good news is that the right first steps are simple, and almost everyone who follows them gets moving again with nothing worse than a story to tell.

We have driven the Ring Road ourselves, and our own nine-day trip, which you can read about in our Iceland Ring Road story, had its share of surprises. This guide pulls together what we have learned, what rental companies commonly ask you to do, and the advice that official Icelandic sources give to visitors. It is written for ordinary travellers, not mechanics, so you do not need any special skills. Treat it as a calm checklist you can read before you go and again if something goes wrong.

What to Do in the First Ten Minutes

The first ten minutes matter more than anything that follows. Work through these five steps in order. You will notice that fixing the van is not on the list yet. Safety, communication and a clear record come first.

  1. Get safe and visible. Ease off the accelerator, signal and roll to the safest spot you can reach. A gravel pull-out or wide shoulder is far better than a bend or the crest of a hill. Switch on your hazard lights, apply the handbrake and, if you have to stand outside, wear something bright and stay well away from passing traffic. In strong wind, open doors slowly and hold them firmly, because gusts can bend or tear a door, and wind damage is often excluded from rental cover.
  2. Decide whether it is an emergency. Call 112 if anyone is hurt, if the vehicle is in a dangerous position, if you see smoke or fire, or if you are stuck somewhere remote while the weather worsens. 112 is Iceland's emergency number for police, ambulance, fire and rescue, and you can learn more on the official 112 site. If you are unsure, call and let them decide. They would much rather hear from you early.
  3. Call your rental company. For an ordinary mechanical problem, this is your first call. The 24-hour number should be in your rental papers. Tell them your name, the registration number, what happened and exactly where you are. They will tell you whether to try a simple fix, wait for roadside help or arrange a replacement. Do not pay a garage or tow operator until the company approves it, because paying without approval can make refunds complicated later.
  4. Document everything. While you wait, take photos of the vehicle, the dashboard, the surroundings and any damage. Write down the time, your location, what you did and who you spoke to. Keep every receipt. If you later claim on rental or travel insurance, this record is your best friend.
  5. Stay warm, stay put and wait smart. Stay with the vehicle unless you are certain that walking is safer, because a campervan is far easier for rescuers to spot than a person. Keep your layers on, drink water, protect your phone battery and share your location with someone you trust.

Common Campervan Problems and What to Do

Most breakdowns fall into a handful of categories. Open each one below for a plain-language guide. In every case, the first call is to your rental company unless someone is in danger.

Flat tyre

Pull over somewhere flat and safe, switch on your hazards and call your rental company before you do anything else. Some vans carry a spare and a jack, others carry a repair kit, and some companies prefer that you wait for help. Gravel is a common cause, so ask at pick-up whether tyre damage is covered. Take photos of the tyre and the road surface.

Van will not start or the battery is flat

Check that lights, the fridge, the heater and chargers were not left running overnight, because cold weather drains batteries quickly. Many campervans have a separate leisure battery, so find out at pick-up which one powers what. If the engine will not turn over, do not keep cranking it. Call your rental company, who may talk you through a jump start or send help.

Warning light or overheating

Never ignore a warning light. Note the symbol and take a photo of the dashboard. If the engine is overheating or you smell burning, stop safely, switch off the engine and wait. Never open a hot coolant cap. Then call the rental company with the symbol and what the van was doing when it appeared.

Out of fuel or the wrong fuel

Fuel stations can be far apart in remote areas, so top up when you can. If you run out, stay safe and call your rental company. If you put in the wrong fuel, do not start the engine, because that can cause serious damage. Tell the company straight away. At pick-up, confirm whether the van takes diesel or petrol and make a note of it.

Stuck in mud, sand or snow

Stop spinning the wheels, which only digs you in deeper. If it is safe, clear snow from around the tyres, but do not wander far from the van. If you are on an unsuitable road or the weather is getting worse, call 112. Remember that many rental agreements exclude damage from driving on roads the vehicle is not allowed on.

Wind or gravel damage

Strong gusts can bend doors and strip paint, and passing cars can chip windscreens. Take photos of everything, call the rental company and ask what is covered. Extras such as gravel protection or sand and ash protection exist for exactly this reason, so check what you bought and what the exclusions say.

Why Campervans Break Down in Iceland (and Why Most Are Preventable)

Iceland is wonderful to drive, but it asks more of a vehicle than a typical holiday road. Understanding the usual causes helps you avoid most of them.

  • Gravel and rough surfaces. Loose stones cause chipped windscreens, flat tyres and cracked lights. Slow down on gravel and give oncoming traffic plenty of room.
  • Long gaps between services. Running low on fuel on a quiet stretch is a classic mistake. Refuel before long empty sections rather than waiting for the warning light.
  • Fast-changing weather. Wind, rain, ice and snow can arrive within an hour and turn a simple stop into a difficult one.
  • Electrical drain. Heaters, fridges, chargers and lights can empty a battery overnight, especially in the cold.
  • Unsuitable roads and river crossings. F-roads are mountain tracks where only suitable four-wheel-drive vehicles are allowed, and river crossings can wreck an engine. A standard campervan should stay away from both.
  • Everyday habits. Slamming doors open in wind, ignoring a small warning light or skipping the pick-up inspection all turn small issues into big ones.

The takeaway is encouraging. Most breakdowns come from a few predictable situations, so a little preparation goes a long way.

Insurance, Costs and Who Pays

Money worries often make a stressful moment worse, so it helps to understand the basics before you travel. We are not insurance advisers, and every rental company and policy is different, so use this as a guide to the right questions rather than as advice about your own cover.

Rental agreements in Iceland commonly include collision damage cover with an excess, and offer extras such as gravel protection, sand and ash protection and theft protection. These extras exist because certain types of damage are common and are often excluded from the basic package. River crossings, driving on roads your vehicle is not allowed on, and wind damage to doors are examples that many agreements treat separately. Read the exclusions line by line, and ask at pick-up what happens if you break down: who sends help, who pays for towing and whether a replacement vehicle is provided.

Your travel insurance is a second layer. Some policies cover rental vehicle excess or trip disruption, while others do not. If you are travelling from India and applying for a Schengen visa, you will need travel medical insurance for the visa itself, and a visa-compliant policy does not automatically cover vehicle problems. Our international travel insurance guide for Indians explains what to look for, and our Visa Guide helps you plan the paperwork early.

Keep your paperwork togetherPut your rental agreement, insurance documents and emergency numbers in one folder on your phone and one on paper. In a stressful moment, you will be glad you did.

A Message to Send Your Rental Company

When you are cold and worried, it is easy to forget the details the company needs. Keep the message below on your phone, fill in the brackets and send it by text or email while you call. A written record also helps if there is a dispute about costs later.

Hello, this is [your name] with campervan [registration number and model].

We have broken down and need help.

Location: [route number, nearest town or landmark, GPS coordinates, direction of travel]
Problem: [what happened, any warning light or noise]
Is everyone safe? [yes / no]
Is the van in a safe spot? [yes / no]
Fuel type: [diesel / petrol]. Fuel level: [approximate]
Phone number to reach me: [number]
Phone battery left: [percentage]

Please tell me what to do next and whether you approve any repair or towing costs.

To describe your location clearly, give the road number first, then the nearest town, farm or landmark, then the direction you were travelling. Add the GPS coordinates from your phone's map. If you cannot say where you are, say what you can see, such as a bridge, a sign or a distinctive mountain, and send a photo.

Staying Safe While You Wait

Help can take time in a country with long distances and few people, so plan to wait comfortably. A safe wait is part of fixing the problem.

  • Stay with the van. It is shelter, it is visible and it holds your supplies. Walk for help only if you are certain it is safer than waiting.
  • Keep warm. Put on dry layers, a hat and gloves. Use blankets and sleeping bags from the van.
  • Mind the exhaust. If you run the engine for heat, make sure the exhaust pipe is not blocked by snow, because exhaust fumes are dangerous in an enclosed space.
  • Save phone power. Lower the screen brightness, close unused apps and use a power bank for the calls that matter.
  • Eat and drink. Keep snacks and water within reach. Warm drinks from a flask help morale as well as temperature.
  • Watch the weather. If conditions turn dangerous, call 112 rather than waiting for the rental company to call back.

Mistakes to Avoid

  • Paying for repairs or towing before the rental company has approved them.
  • Starting the engine after using the wrong fuel.
  • Leaving the vehicle in a storm because you think you can walk to help.
  • Driving a standard campervan on an F-road or across a river.
  • Ignoring a warning light because the van still seems to drive normally.
  • Opening doors in strong wind without holding them.
  • Not taking photos or notes, then struggling to explain what happened.
  • Relying on a single phone with a low battery and no offline numbers.

Summer Versus Winter Breakdowns

The season changes what can go wrong, and how quickly it matters.

In summer

Long daylight and milder weather make waiting easier, but traffic is heavier and gravel roads are busy, so chipped windscreens and flat tyres are common. Mountain roads open later in the season, and swollen rivers can tempt drivers into mistakes. Wait for help rather than trying a crossing.

In winter

Short days, ice, snow and wind raise the stakes. Batteries struggle in the cold, roads can close at short notice and a wait can become dangerous quickly. Check road.is and the weather forecast every morning, carry extra warm clothing and call early if conditions are worsening.

Final Thoughts

An Iceland campervan breakdown is stressful, but it is rarely a disaster if you stay calm and follow a clear order: get safe, call 112 only if there is danger, call your rental company for mechanical problems, document everything and wait sensibly. Preparation before you leave does most of the work for you.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who do I call if my campervan breaks down in Iceland?

Call 112 first if anyone is hurt or you are in danger. For a mechanical problem, call the 24-hour emergency number from your rental company, which is normally in your rental paperwork. Don't arrange or pay for repairs before the company approves them.

What is the emergency number in Iceland?

112 is the emergency number for police, ambulance, fire and rescue. It works from any phone with network coverage, and the 112 Iceland app can share your location with responders.

Does insurance cover a campervan breakdown in Iceland?

It depends on the cause and your policy. Many rental agreements exclude damage from river crossings, unsuitable roads, wind, sand, ash and gravel unless you bought extra protection. Read your agreement before you leave and ask what roadside help and towing are included.

What should I do if I get a flat tyre or run out of fuel in Iceland?

Pull over safely, switch on your hazard lights and call your rental company. They will tell you whether to change the tyre yourself or wait for help. If you used the wrong fuel, don't restart the engine and call them straight away.

Can I sleep in my campervan if I break down in Iceland?

If you are stranded and it is unsafe to move, staying with the vehicle is often the safest choice. Keep warm, keep the exhaust clear if you run the engine, and tell your rental company or 112 if conditions are serious. On ordinary nights, campervans generally need to stay at designated campsites.

How can I reduce the risk of a campervan breakdown in Iceland?

Check tyres, fluids, lights and fuel at pick-up, refuel before long empty stretches, stay on roads your vehicle is allowed on, watch road.is and the weather forecast, and register your plans at SafeTravel.is.
