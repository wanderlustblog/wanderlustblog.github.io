Bali Beyond the Instagram: The Real Island Awaits

March 20, 202610 min readby Priya Nair

Deep in the highlands, past the tourist circuits, is a Bali that moves at the pace of a rice farmer's day.

Canggu and Seminyak get most of the attention online, but the Bali that actually stayed with us is further north and inland, in the highland villages around Sidemen and Munduk, where rice terraces do the talking instead of beach clubs and the pace slows to something closer to a farmer's actual day.

Sidemen over Ubud

Ubud has become its own kind of crowded in the years since it became a wellness-tourism byword — traffic jams on streets barely wide enough for one car, cafes with hour-long waits for a smoothie bowl. Sidemen, an hour further east along winding roads, offers the same emerald terrace views with a fraction of the traffic, and homestays run by families who've farmed the same land for generations rather than businesses built specifically around the tourist wave.

We stayed with a family whose youngest son, maybe nineteen, walked us through the terraces one evening explaining the traditional subak irrigation system that's kept these hillsides productive for centuries. It's a genuinely clever piece of communal engineering, and hearing it explained by someone whose family actually relies on it hit differently than reading about it in a guidebook.

Slow mornings, real Bali

Waking with the farmers around 6am, watching the terraces mist over before the heat of the day sets in, understanding a little of why this island earned its reputation long before Instagram got involved — that rhythm is available almost anywhere outside the main tourist strip, if you're willing to trade nightlife for it.

Munduk's waterfalls and coffee

Further north still, Munduk sits at a higher elevation, noticeably cooler, surrounded by clove and coffee plantations rather than rice. The waterfalls here see far fewer visitors than the famous ones near Ubud, and several of the small coffee farms offer informal tours that end, predictably and pleasantly, with a cup brewed from beans picked that same morning.

Temples away from the crowds

Pura Luhur Batukaru, tucked into dense forest on the slopes of Mount Batukaru, receives a fraction of the visitors that Tanah Lot or Uluwatu see daily. Moss-covered stone, quiet enough to hear birds clearly, no queue for photographs — it felt like a genuinely different category of experience from the more famous temple sites.

What the highlands taught us about the coast

Spending most of our time inland gave us a different lens for the two days we eventually did spend near the coast — the beach clubs and surf breaks felt less like an obligation to check off and more like a genuine change of pace after a week of quiet villages, rather than the default version of Bali we'd have experienced by starting there.

Getting around

A scooter works well for the shorter hops between highland villages if you're comfortable riding on narrower, occasionally rough roads. For longer stretches, particularly with luggage, a hired driver for the day was worth the modest extra cost — Balinese roads reward local knowledge more than most.

The real Bali was never actually gone. It's just a little further from the main road than most itineraries bother to travel, and it rewards exactly the kind of unhurried curiosity that the beach clubs further south don't really ask of you.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Sidemen hard to reach?

It's roughly a 1.5–2 hour drive from Ubud on winding roads — manageable by scooter or hired car.

Is Bali's dry season crowded everywhere?

The south coast gets busy, but highland villages like Sidemen and Munduk stay noticeably quieter year-round.

Do I need a guide for the rice terraces?

No — most are open to independent walking, though a local guide adds context on the subak irrigation system.
