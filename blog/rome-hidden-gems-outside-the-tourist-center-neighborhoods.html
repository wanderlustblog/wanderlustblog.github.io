Rome rewards the visitor who walks a little further. The Colosseum, the Vatican and the Trevi Fountain are worth seeing, but the city beyond the historic centre is where trattorias fill with locals, street art covers old gasworks and a hill of broken Roman pottery sits quietly behind a neighbourhood market. Most of these places are a short metro, tram or bus ride away.

This guide groups the neighbourhoods that published guides recommend outside the tourist centre, with what each offers, how to fit them into a day and what to watch out for. A note on accuracyOur sources include Lonely Planet, Rough Guides, an Afar piece, a tour company blog (Walks of Italy) and a Yahoo travel article that appears to date from about 2014. None gives opening hours or ticket prices for most places, and the tour company promotes its own tours. Restaurants, markets and museums change, so check each one before you go.

What 'Outside the Tourist Centre' Means in Rome

Afar treats the area inside the Aurelian Walls as the historic centre and suggests exploring neighbourhoods outside it, and it advises staying longer, five days or a week, to see beyond the main sights. That is a useful frame. You can still enjoy the famous sights, then spend a day or two in places where the crowds thin out.

Not every place on these lists is truly hidden. Walks of Italy itself calls only Coppedè slightly hidden, and describes Trastevere as popular and Parioli as elegant. Treat 'hidden' as 'less crowded than the centre', not as undiscovered.

Neighbourhoods at a Glance

Use the filter to compare. Everything below comes from the sources named in the accuracy note.

Testaccio

South of the centreFood-focused

Named in every source. Monte Testaccio is a hill of discarded Roman pottery, and the neighbourhood has traditional trattorias such as Felice a Testaccio and Flavio al Velavevodetto, plus a market and aperitivo bars. Book dinner in advance, says Walks of Italy. The Protestant Cemetery and the Pyramid of Cestius are nearby.

Ostiense

Next to TestaccioStreet art

An industrial district known for large murals, including Blu's mural on Via Porto Fluviale, and for Eataly in the railway station. One source compares it to Williamsburg.

Garbatella

Southern RomeGarden-city streets

Curvy 1920s housing, courtyards, community gardens and trattorias at local prices, according to Rough Guides. Centrale Montemartini, a former power plant displaying Roman sculptures among turbines, is nearby and described as less crowded than the Capitoline museums.

Pigneto

About 25 minutes from the centreArty

A district with bars, cafes, arthouse cinemas and music venues, less crowded with tourists, according to Walks of Italy. Afar calls it up and coming.

Quartiere Coppedè

North RomeFantasy architecture

About 47 eclectic buildings blending Art Nouveau, Art Deco and Gothic or Baroque elements, built in 1913 to 1927, with the Fountain of the Frogs. Walks of Italy warns there are few cafes and restaurants, so eat beforehand.

Aventine Hill

South of the Circus MaximusCalm

Orange Garden with views over the Tiber and St Peter's, the Santa Sabina door and the Knights of Malta keyhole that frames St Peter's dome. Yahoo's article suggests spring, when the gardens bloom.

Monti

Near the ColosseumVillage feel

A bohemian area with small businesses, thrift shops, and the Piazza della Madonna dei Monti fountain, per Walks of Italy. Its location next to the Colosseum means it is more central than the others.

Monteverde

West of TrasteverePizza

Rough Guides names La Gatta Mangiona here for 72-hour dough pizza. It says to book ahead, and notes it is popular with Romans.

EUR

Southern Rome1940s architecture

Architecture built for a 1942 World Fair that was cancelled, including the Palazzo della Civiltà Italiana. The Museo della Civiltà has a cast of Trajan's Column and a scale model of ancient Rome, according to the older article.

Esquilino and San Lorenzo

Near TerminiDiverse and arty

Afar describes Esquilino as diverse with bakeries and restaurants around Piazza Vittorio, and San Lorenzo as filled with street art. Lonely Planet warns the area around Termini can feel unsafe at night.

Appia Antica and parks

SouthOpen space

The ancient road is explored by bike or guided tour, and Rough Guides says it is closed to traffic on Sundays. Parco degli Acquedotti and Parco della Caffarella are green spaces, with the former good for sunset walks.

Three Easy Day Plans

Open the tab that matches your style. These are our own suggestions built from the places above, not tested routes.

Food day

Testaccio and OstienseSlow

Start with coffee and a pastry in Testaccio, then visit the market and Monte Testaccio. Book a traditional trattoria for lunch or dinner, and walk to Ostiense for street art and an aperitivo. Our sources give no opening hours, so check the market and restaurants first.

Art and architecture day

Garbatella and CoppedèTwo corners of Rome

Visit Centrale Montemartini and Garbatella's courtyards in the morning. Head north in the afternoon to Quartiere Coppedè, and eat beforehand because the area has few cafes. It is a long journey across the city, so check transport on a map.

Green and views day

Aventine and Appia AnticaGentle

Walk the Aventine for the Orange Garden and the keyhole. On a Sunday, consider the Appia Antica when it is closed to traffic, hiring a bike, and finish in Parco degli Acquedotti for sunset.

Eating Like a Local Outside the Centre

Afar gives a useful list of signs that a restaurant is a tourist trap: it sits on a main piazza, has photo menus, has staff soliciting you or makes fresh pasta in the window. It also advises booking instead of queuing, because traditional restaurants often take reservations for the whole evening, and names Al Pompiere, La Matriciana dal 1870 and Flavio al Velavevodetto as old-school options. Romans generally do not queue, except at pizzerias and street food spots, according to the same article.

Outside the centre, you will also find neighbourhood markets and bakeries. Rough Guides lists Mercato Trionfale as a working market with more than 270 stalls and take-away foods such as supplì and porchetta. Check hours before you go.

Getting Around and Staying Comfortable

Our sources give few transport details. Lonely Planet describes Flaminio as quiet and well connected by metro and bus, and says the Termini area is walkable with two metro lines and many buses but can feel unsafe at night, while Piazza della Repubblica nearby is calmer. Walks of Italy suggests taxis or ride apps late at night in Parioli. Lonely Planet also suggests underground sites such as catacombs and Nero's Domus Aurea during summer heat, noting that not all catacombs are open to the public, and mentions Ostia Antica by the Metro Mare train.

  • Use a map app to check travel times across the city.
  • Wear comfortable shoes, since cobbles and long walks are common.
  • Take normal precautions near stations and at night.
  • Check opening days, because many museums and churches close on set days. Our sources do not list them.

Heat, Rain and Crowd Timing

Rome gets hot, and several of these places are outdoors. Lonely Planet suggests underground sites such as the catacombs and the crypts at San Clemente in summer heat, which makes a good swap for a midday walk. On a rainy day, indoor options include Centrale Montemartini, Palazzo Spada and the Baths of Diocletian, all named in our sources.

  • Walk early or late in summer, and rest in the middle of the day.
  • Carry water and a small umbrella or light rain jacket.
  • Visit churches with covered shoulders and knees, since many require modest dress, though our sources do not list rules.
  • Avoid the biggest sights at midday and use that time for a neighbourhood lunch instead.

Quieter Museums and Churches

If the centre feels crowded, Afar suggests Palazzo Massimo and the Baths of Diocletian, with a combined Museo Nazionale Romano ticket listed at 16 euros, as well as Palazzo Doria Pamphilj, Palazzo Colonna and Villa Farnesina. That price is from an article of unknown date, so check it. Rough Guides adds Palazzo Spada, with Borromini's forced-perspective gallery, and the Basilica of Saint Praxedes with its Byzantine mosaics. None of these is guaranteed to be empty, but they are quieter than the big names.

Final Thoughts

Rome outside the centre is a city of markets, murals, hills and courtyards, where lunch is an event and the crowds are thinner. Pick a neighbourhood that suits your mood, book your dinner, check your transport and allow time to wander. The famous sights will still be there afterwards.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which Rome neighbourhoods are best outside the tourist centre?

Guides frequently name Testaccio, Ostiense, Garbatella, Pigneto, Quartiere Coppedè and the Aventine Hill, plus Monti, Monteverde and EUR in some lists.

Is Testaccio worth visiting?

Every source we read recommends it. It has traditional trattorias, a market, Monte Testaccio and the Protestant Cemetery. Book dinner in advance.

Is Quartiere Coppedè worth the trip?

It has unusual early 20th-century architecture and the Fountain of the Frogs, but few cafes or restaurants, so eat beforehand.

How do I avoid tourist-trap restaurants in Rome?

Afar advises avoiding places on main piazzas, with photo menus, soliciting staff or fresh pasta made in the window, and booking traditional restaurants instead of queuing.

Is the area near Termini safe?

Lonely Planet says the area around the station can feel unsafe at night, while nearby Piazza della Repubblica is calmer. Take normal precautions.

Can I visit the Appian Way on foot or by bike?

Rough Guides says it is closed to traffic on Sundays and bikes can be rented. Lonely Planet suggests e-bikes or guided tours past the Villa of Maxentius and the Claudian Aqueduct.
