Japan is possible for Jain travellers, but it takes more planning than almost any other popular destination. Seafood runs through the cuisine, dashi fish stock hides in soups and sauces, and onion, garlic and root vegetables are everywhere. The good news is that a handful of kitchens, a few temple meals and some smart self-catering can make a Japan trip workable, and many Jain families have done it.

This guide explains the main obstacles, shows the options that appear in published guides, gives you a written request you can adapt and offers a plan for each city. If you are still deciding where to go, our vegetarian food in Kyoto for Indian travelers guide and our Tokyo neighborhoods guide cover the wider vegetarian picture, and our Japan visa checklist covers the paperwork.

Why Jain Food Is Hard in Japan

Three things make Japan tough. First, seafood: the Pickyourtrail guide says Japanese cuisine has a seafood focus. Second, dashi: "Anything with dashi usually includes fish," says the same guide, which suggests kombu or shiitake broths instead. Third, aromatics and roots: onion, garlic and root vegetables are common in Japanese cooking.

Shojin ryori, the Buddhist temple cuisine, sounds like the answer, and it helps, but it is not automatically Jain. The Zurich Kotak guide says it does not automatically suit a Jain diet and advises giving the temple your full list. Pickyourtrail adds that you should confirm onion and garlic exclusions individually.

Your Written Request Card

Both guides recommend carrying a translated request card, and Pickyourtrail suggests keeping a photo of the filled-out card on your phone. The Zurich Kotak guide says to give restaurants, ryokan and temples your full list in writing in advance. A HappyFares page gives one Japanese phrase for "no onion, no garlic": 玉ねぎとニンニク抜きでお願いします. Ask your hotel to check the rest of your card in Japanese.

  1. List what you avoid. Meat, fish, egg, dashi (fish stock), onion, garlic, root vegetables and anything else your tradition excludes.
  2. Add a plain sentence. Say you cannot eat these even in small amounts or in broth.
  3. Translate it. Have the hotel or a reliable translator check the Japanese.
  4. Save a photo. Keep it on your phone, and carry a printed copy.
  5. Send it ahead. Email restaurants, ryokan and temples before you book.

The Zurich Kotak guide also names Japanese label words to check on packaged food: エキス (extract), ゼラチン (gelatin) and 煮干し (dried sardines). It says to read the ingredient list, because allergen symbols do not cover bonito.

Options That Appear in Published Guides

Use the filter to see what is available. Only one restaurant is described as Jain by default, and the rest adapt on request.

Vege Herb Saga

OkachimachiJain by default

HappyFares describes it as a multi-region veg thali with no onion or garlic by default, with an owner who is a practising Jain, and calls it the safest Jain option on its list. Confirm before you go.

Milan Nataraj

ShibuyaJain on request

A long-running pure-veg and vegan Ayurvedic restaurant with a lunch buffet, according to HappyFares. Pickyourtrail also lists Nataraj, in Harajuku and Shibuya, as a fully vegetarian Indian restaurant that offers Jain customisations. Ask for no onion, garlic or root vegetables.

Andhra Dining and Andhra Kitchen

Ginza and OkachimachiJain on request

South Indian veg thali and dosa places. Andhra Kitchen is described as the cheaper sister of Andhra Dining. They adapt only if you ask clearly.

Amudhasurabhi

Nishi-KasaiJain on request

South Indian thali and dosa. HappyFares warns that Nishi-Kasai has many mixed veg and non-veg places, so confirm per restaurant.

Shigetsu at Tenryu-ji

ArashiyamaShojin ryori

Temple cuisine with tofu, seasonal vegetables and kombu or shiitake broths, with reservations recommended. Pickyourtrail says to confirm onion and garlic exclusions individually.

Other Kyoto temple meals

Shojin ryoriGive your full list

Many Kyoto temples serve shojin ryori, but the Zurich Kotak guide says it does not automatically suit a Jain diet. Send your list in advance.

Koyasan temple lodgings

WakayamaBook well ahead

JNTO is quoted as saying each meal at the 51 shukubo is free of meat, fish, green onion and garlic. Ask about root vegetables and dashi in writing.

Ryokan with notice

Cooked to orderTell them at booking

The guide says ryokan can cook vegetarian meals with notice. Give your complete list when you book.

Indian grocers and kitchens

Tokyo, Kyoto, OsakaLonger stays

Pickyourtrail suggests Indian and South Asian grocers for staples such as besan, suji, dals, ghee and Jain snacks, and accommodation with a kitchenette for longer stays.

Local ingredients

Tofu, kombu, dried shiitakeCook simply

Pickyourtrail suggests buying Japanese staples such as tofu, miso, kombu and dried shiitake. Check miso labels for fish stock.

City-by-City Reality Check

  • Tokyo: the best choice, with several Indian vegetarian restaurants that adapt, and Indian grocers. See our Tokyo neighborhoods guide.
  • Kyoto: temple meals help, but confirm every detail. Our Kyoto vegetarian guide covers dashi and where to eat.
  • Osaka: Pickyourtrail says options are growing, with Indian groceries and vegetarian restaurants, but names no specific restaurant. Research before you go.
  • Other cities: Nara, Yokohama and Sapporo are mentioned as growing options with no restaurants named. Plan to self-cater.

Flights and Packing

Pickyourtrail's FAQ says Jain meals are available on request, at least 24 hours before departure, on carriers such as ANA and Japan Airlines. Confirm with your airline. The guide does not advise packing food from home, and the Zurich Kotak guide says Japan restricts meat, vegetable and fruit products at the border, so check with Japan's Animal Quarantine Service and Plant Protection Station before you pack Indian food. Neither guide says whether packaged Indian foods are allowed.

Safe Snacks and Backup Meals

Even with careful planning, you will have days when the plan falls through. A backup stash turns those days into minor inconveniences. The Zurich Kotak guide lists convenience store options such as onigiri with umeboshi or kombu, edamame, packaged beans, simple salads and inarizushi, but it is written for vegetarians in general, so apply your own rules on each ingredient and read the label.

  • Check every label. Use the ingredient list, not just allergen icons, and look for extract, gelatin and dried sardine words.
  • Carry dry snacks. Nuts, fruit and plain biscuits help on travel days. Check border rules before bringing food from India.
  • Use grocery stores. Tofu, fruit, bread and simple salads from supermarkets let you assemble a meal yourself.
  • Keep an Indian restaurant bookmarked near each hotel as your fallback.

Talking to Kitchens and Hosts

Politeness matters. Japanese kitchens take care seriously, and a clear, early request is respected. Send your card in advance, thank the staff and reconfirm on the day. If a restaurant cannot guarantee a dish, accept the answer and choose another place. That honesty is better than a half-promise. For temples and ryokan, write ahead and explain that your diet is a religious practice, and ask what they can and cannot do. Pickyourtrail also suggests that local Jain centres and Indian cultural associations may help verify options.

Mistakes to Avoid

  • Assuming vegetarian restaurants avoid onion and garlic by default.
  • Trusting shojin ryori without confirming aromatics.
  • Not booking, since temples and vegetarian restaurants fill up in cherry blossom season and Golden Week.
  • Relying on an old list. Pickyourtrail advises trusting recent reviews over outdated listings.
  • Ordering miso soup or noodle broth without asking about dashi.
  • Skipping insurance. Read our travel insurance guide for Indians.

Final Thoughts

Jain food in Japan is possible, but it rewards preparation. Choose Tokyo for the most reliable options, confirm every kitchen, carry a clear written request and plan for self-catering. If you accept that some days will be simple, the temples, gardens and trains are still well worth the trip.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can Jains eat in Japan?

It is possible but takes planning. Guides say options are best in Tokyo, Kyoto and Osaka, and that dashi fish stock, onion, garlic and root vegetables are common. Confirm every meal.

Is shojin ryori Jain?

Not automatically. The Zurich Kotak guide says it does not suit a Jain diet by default and advises giving the temple your full list. Confirm onion, garlic and broth.

Are there Jain restaurants in Tokyo?

HappyFares lists Vege Herb Saga as Jain by default, and Milan Nataraj, Andhra Dining, Andhra Kitchen and Amudhasurabhi as Jain on request. Confirm current details.

How do I say no onion and garlic in Japanese?

HappyFares gives 玉ねぎとニンニク抜きでお願いします. Have your hotel check your full request card in Japanese.

Can I get Jain meals on flights to Japan?

Pickyourtrail's FAQ says Jain meals are available on request at least 24 hours before departure on carriers such as ANA and Japan Airlines. Confirm with your airline.

Can I bring Indian food into Japan?

Check first. The Zurich Kotak guide says Japan restricts meat, vegetable and fruit products at the border and points to Japan's Animal Quarantine Service and Plant Protection Station.
