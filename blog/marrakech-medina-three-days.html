Lost in the Medina: Three Days in Marrakech

March 29, 20269 min readby Luca Ferretti

Spice-scented alleys, rooftop mint tea, and the beautiful disorientation of Jemaa el-Fnaa square at dusk.

Getting lost in the Marrakech medina isn't a risk so much as the plan, whether you intend it or not. The alleys aren't built for a straight line anywhere — they twist around old riads, double back on themselves, occasionally dead-end into someone's courtyard — and that's exactly what makes wandering them worthwhile rather than merely disorienting.

The souks, without the sales pitch

Go early, before 9am, when stallholders are still setting up rather than actively pitching. You'll see the medina as a working neighborhood rather than a marketplace performing for tourists — leather being dyed, spices being weighed out for local restaurants, metalwork being hammered into shape in workshops barely wider than a doorway. By the time the main tourist flow arrives around 10am, the whole rhythm of the place shifts.

We spent one entire morning just following the sound of hammering, which led us to a small metalworking workshop where an older craftsman let us watch him finish a lantern that had clearly taken days of work already. He didn't speak much English, we didn't speak much Arabic, and none of that mattered much in the end.

Jemaa el-Fnaa at dusk

The square transforms completely after sunset — food stalls rolling in on carts, storytellers gathering small crowds, drummers setting up in loose circles, the whole space filling with smoke and noise and motion. A rooftop cafe overlooking it all with a glass of mint tea is one of the simplest, best ways to spend an hour in Morocco, and we did it on all three nights, each time finding something new happening below.

A day trip worth the early start

The Ourika Valley, roughly an hour outside the city in the foothills of the Atlas Mountains, offered a completely different pace after two days of medina chaos — Berber villages, waterfalls, and air that felt noticeably cooler and cleaner than the city below. It's an easy half-day trip if you arrange transport the night before through your riad.

Food beyond the square

Jemaa el-Fnaa's food stalls are worth trying once, mostly for the atmosphere, but some of the best meals we had were in small, unmarked family restaurants a few streets back from the main tourist flow, recommended to us by our riad host rather than found in any guide. One place, essentially someone's front room with four tables, served a lamb tagine that remains one of the best meals either of us can remember from any trip.

Where to stay

We stayed in a small riad inside the medina walls rather than a hotel outside them, and it made a real difference — waking up to the call to prayer, the courtyard fountain, breakfast served on a rooftop terrace overlooking a maze of other rooftops. It costs more attention to navigate to and from (our riad owner drew us a hand map on our first night, which we used constantly) but the atmosphere is worth the extra ten minutes of navigation each time.

What surprised us most

How genuinely welcoming most interactions were, once we got past the handful of overly persistent guides near the main entrances. Away from the busiest tourist corridors, people were patient with our terrible French, happy to point us in the right direction, and generally uninterested in selling us anything at all.

Three days is enough to fall for Marrakech's particular kind of organized chaos. It's not enough to fully understand it, and that's fine — that gap is exactly what brings people back for a second visit.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it easy to get lost in the medina?

Very — that's normal even for residents. Save your riad's location offline and it stops being stressful.

Do I need to haggle in the souks?

Bargaining is standard practice; starting around half the initial asking price is a common approach.

Is three days enough for Marrakech?

Enough for the medina and a day trip, though many extend to combine it with the Atlas Mountains or a desert excursion.
