Norway's Fjords: A Summer Road Trip Through Scandinavia's Soul

January 10, 202611 min readby Maya Chen

Hairpin roads cut through dramatic cliffs above mirror-calm fjords. In summer, Norway has no darkness — only endless golden light.

Driving the Norwegian fjord country in June means the sun barely sets, and the light does strange, beautiful things to the water over the course of a single day — mirror-flat and silver in the early morning, wind-rippled gold by early evening, and somehow still bright enough at midnight to read a map by.

The Trollstigen detour

Eleven hairpin bends up a mountainside that looks genuinely impossible from below and somehow isn't, Trollstigen is exactly the kind of white-knuckle drive that pays off entirely at the top. The viewpoint over the valley below, with waterfalls visible on both sides of the road, is one of the most dramatic road-trip moments either of us has experienced anywhere in Europe, and we sat there far longer than the parking situation probably allowed.

Sognefjord, slowly

Norway's longest fjord deserves at least two unhurried days rather than a single rushed transit — small ferry crossings between towns that appear to exist mostly for the ferry itself, quiet fishing villages with populations you could count on two hands, and glacier views that appear suddenly around a bend and disappear behind cloud just as quickly. We took a small detour up a side arm of the fjord on a recommendation from a guesthouse owner and found a waterfall with no name we could find on any map, no other visitors, and nothing to do but sit and watch it for twenty minutes.

Bergen's bookend

Starting the trip in Bergen gave us a soft entry before the more demanding mountain driving further north — colorful wooden buildings along the historic wharf, a genuinely excellent fish market, and a funicular up Mount Fløyen for a first orientation view over the fjords surrounding the city. It's touristy in a way the rest of the route generally isn't, but earns it.

Geirangerfjord's crowds, and how to avoid them

Geirangerfjord is arguably the most photographed fjord in the country, and the cruise ship crowds that follow can be genuinely overwhelming during midday in peak season. Arriving early or staying overnight in the small village rather than day-tripping in gave us a version of the fjord — quiet water, soft light, none of the ship-passenger crush — that felt entirely different from what most day visitors experience.

The cost of it all

Fuel and food cost noticeably more here than almost anywhere else in Europe we've traveled, and we adjusted by self-catering more than we'd originally planned, buying groceries in larger towns and eating simply on the road most days. It's worth budgeting for this in advance rather than being surprised by it a few days in, the way we initially were.

Midnight light, still driving

More than once we found ourselves still on the road at what would normally be well past dark, simply because the sun hadn't fully set and the light was too good to stop for the night. It reshapes how you plan each day entirely — "how much daylight is left" stops being a real constraint for much of the trip.

Ending in Oslo

Finishing the loop in Oslo felt like a deliberate change of pace after so much mountain driving — a proper city again, museums, a harbor to walk along without needing to watch for oncoming traffic on a single-lane road. It's not the dramatic climax the fjords themselves provide, but as a place to decompress before flying home, it worked well.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to book ferries in advance?

Some smaller fjord ferries run on a fixed schedule without booking; larger routes in peak summer are worth reserving ahead.

Is Norway really that expensive?

Yes — food, fuel, and accommodation run notably higher than most of Europe; budgeting extra is wise.

Is driving in Norway difficult for visitors?

Roads are well maintained but narrow and winding in fjord country — manageable at a relaxed pace, less so if rushed.
