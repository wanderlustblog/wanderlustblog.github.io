What a Paid Guest Post on a Travel Blog Actually Is

A paid guest post is an article published on someone else's blog in exchange for a fee. The article is written for readers first, usually about a destination, an activity, a safety topic or a planning problem, and it may mention or link to the sponsor's product or service. Because money changes hands, it is advertising, and it should be treated and labelled that way.

That does not make it low quality. The best sponsored posts are genuinely useful: a practical guide that a reader would be glad to find, with a clearly marked place where the sponsor's offer appears. The worst are thin pages stuffed with links. Readers and search engines both notice the difference.

How Paid Links Should Be Labelled

Google's documentation on qualifying outbound links says to use rel="sponsored" for links that are ads or paid placements. It notes that nofollow was previously recommended for these links and is still acceptable, but sponsored is preferred. You can combine values, and linked pages may still be crawled through other routes such as sitemaps or links from other sites.

rel="sponsored"

For ads and paid placements. This is the value Google prefers for paid links, and it is what we use on sponsored content.

rel="nofollow"

A general value for links you do not want to associate your site with. It remains acceptable for paid links but is not the preferred choice.

rel="ugc"

For user-generated content such as comments and forum posts. It is not meant for paid placements.

The page we read points to Google's separate spam policies for its position on paid links, and it does not say what happens if you do not qualify them or how the values affect ranking. So we do not make claims about either. We simply label paid links as Google recommends and ask partners to expect that.

Why Disclosure Matters

In the United States, the Federal Trade Commission's native advertising guide says content that promotes a product but is not readily identifiable as an ad may be deceptive, and that a misleadingly formatted ad can be deceptive even if its product claims are true. The guide says terms such as "Ad", "Advertisement", "Paid Advertisement" and "Sponsored Advertising Content" are likely to be understood, and that "Promoted" is ambiguous and should be avoided. It also advises placing the disclosure immediately before or above the headline and making it large and easy to read.

The guide dates from 2015 and has no safe harbour, and rules differ outside the US, so check the rules that apply to you. Our approach is simple: clear labelling near the top of the page, written in plain words.

What this means for youIf you are paying for a post, expect a visible sponsored label. If you are a writer submitting one on behalf of a client, tell us who the sponsor is. Hidden advertising helps nobody.

How a Paid Guest Post Works, Step by Step

The process is short. We do not publish a fixed timeline because it depends on the topic and the back-and-forth, but the steps are always the same.

  1. Send a short pitch. Email us with your business, the topic you have in mind and the page you want to link to.
  2. We check the fit. The topic must help travellers, match our subjects and link to a real product or service page.
  3. Agree scope and price. We confirm whether it is a guest post at $100 or a link insertion at $50, and what is included.
  4. Draft and review. The article is written or supplied, then edited for clarity and usefulness. We may ask for changes.
  5. Publish with labels. The post goes live in our theme with a sponsored label, and paid links carry rel="sponsored".
  6. Share and measure honestly. You can share the live link and track results with your own analytics.

What Makes a Good Pitch

Pitches that get a quick yes share a few traits.

  • A reader-first topic. Think "how to avoid X" or "what to know before Y", not "why our product is great".
  • A real destination or traveller problem. Our posts cover practical topics such as navigation, safety, budgets and planning. See our Marrakech medina offline map tips for the style.
  • A link to a product or service page. We link to your actual page, not to a tracking redirect.
  • Accurate facts. If you cite prices, rules or statistics, give us the source so we can check it.
  • Clear sponsor details. Tell us the company name and your contact.

What You Can Honestly Expect

  • Yes: a well-written, useful article on a travel blog, a labelled link to your page and a post you can share.
  • Maybe: referral visits from readers who find the article interesting, depending on the topic and how readers find it.
  • No guarantee: search rankings, traffic numbers, enquiries or sales. We do not promise these, and neither should anyone selling you a post.
Red flags when buying any paid postGuaranteed rankings, requests to hide the sponsorship, pressure to use exact-match keyword anchors in every link, or posts that have nothing to do with travel. Walk away.

Topic Ideas That Suit a Travel Blog

The strongest sponsored posts answer a question travellers are already asking. Here are the kinds of angles that work, with the sort of business each suits. These are ideas, not a menu, and we will help you shape one.

Practical how-to

Packing, navigation, budgeting or safety guides for a destination. Suits tour operators, gear brands and travel-tech tools.

Destination deep dive

A focused guide to a region, neighbourhood or season. Suits hotels, guesthouses and local experience providers.

Planning checklist

Step-by-step planning for a trip type, such as a first road trip or a winter holiday. Suits rental companies and insurers.

Comparison or chooser

Helping readers pick between options honestly, with trade-offs stated. Suits service providers who are confident in their offer.

Notice what is missing: pure product descriptions. Readers skip them, and so do search engines. Our guides on Iceland campervan parking rules and slow travel in Kyoto for seniors show the level of detail and honesty we aim for.

Link Placement and Anchor Text

Where a link sits and what it says both matter. A link that appears naturally inside a helpful paragraph reads as a recommendation. A link forced into every second sentence reads as spam.

  • Use descriptive anchor text. "Winter campervan rentals in Iceland" tells a reader what they will find. "Click here" does not.
  • Avoid keyword stuffing. One or two natural links per post is plenty. We may trim extra links.
  • Link to a real page. A product or service page that matches the topic, not a tracking redirect.
  • Keep the page live. If your page moves or closes, tell us so we can update the link.
  • Expect a label. The link carries rel="sponsored" and the post carries a visible sponsored label.

Our Quality Standards for Paid Posts

Paid does not mean lower standards. Every sponsored post goes through the same checks as our own stories.

  • The topic must help a traveller and fit the subjects the blog covers.
  • Facts, prices and rules must be accurate and, where possible, sourced.
  • The tone is human, plain and practical, not promotional.
  • Images carry descriptive alt text, and the page works well on a phone.
  • We may decline or edit a post that does not meet these standards, and we will explain why.
