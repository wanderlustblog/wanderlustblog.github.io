Best Hiking Trails Near Hallstatt, Austria for Beginners

August 12, 20269 min readWanderLust Blog

Everyone photographs Hallstatt from the water. These trails show you why the village earned the view in the first place.

Key takeaways

  • All five trails are easy to moderate and need no technical gear.
  • Late May to early October is the best window, with June and September ideal.
  • Only the Salzberg and Krippenstein routes need a cable car ticket.
  • Start early, pack layers, and check the forecast before you go up.

Hallstatt gets treated as a two-hour photo stop by most day-trippers, which is honestly a waste of the surrounding terrain. The village sits inside the Salzkammergut, a region laced with lake-view trails that range from flat lakeside strolls to short alpine climbs — and almost none of them require any real hiking background.

Here are five that consistently deliver a good return for beginner-level effort, ordered from easiest to most demanding.

Start with the shoreline

The Lake Hallstatt Shoreline Path is about as easy as trails get — flat, well-signed, and mostly shaded. It's the right choice for a first afternoon to get oriented, and it ends near a few lakeside cafés that make a good excuse to sit down before tackling anything steeper.

Waldbachstrub: the quiet detour

Most visitors skip this one entirely, which is exactly why it's worth doing. A short forest trail leads to a genuinely pretty waterfall with almost no one else around, even in peak July. Round trip is under an hour, making it easy to fit alongside the shoreline walk on the same day.

The Salzberg cable car isn't cheating — it's the standard way locals reach the higher trailheads, and it saves roughly 40 minutes of steep switchbacks that add little to the view.

Stepping up: Salzberg and Echernwand

The Salzberg Skywalk loop takes the cable car up first, then a short, well-built walking path to the famous viewing platform over the lake. From there, the Echernwand Ridge Path continues along the cliffline for those who want a longer walk with the payoff of an elevated, uninterrupted panorama of Hallstatt below.

For a bigger day: Krippenstein

This is the most demanding of the five, but still manageable for a fit beginner — mostly wide alpine paths rather than technical scrambling. The Austrian alpine club maintains the route well, and the plateau at the top opens onto views across the Dachstein range that make the extra climb worth it.

How to Choose Your Trail

If this is your first time hiking in the mountains, the easiest way to choose is to match the trail to the time and energy you actually have, rather than to the most impressive photo. A rainy morning or a late start suits the Shoreline Path or Waldbachstrub, both of which stay low and are easy to cut short. A clear, settled day is the one to spend on the cable car routes, because the whole point of going up is the view, and cloud sitting on the lake can make a high trail feel like a wasted ticket.

It also helps to be honest about how your legs feel. The distances in the table look short, but time on your feet adds up once you include photo stops, a lunch break, and the walk back down. As a rough guide, plan for the moderate trails to take longer than the distance alone suggests, and leave a buffer so you're never racing the last cable car down. If you're travelling with children or older relatives, the three easy trails are the safest bet, and each has plenty of places to turn around early.

Getting to the Trailheads

Hallstatt is small, and most visitors arrive by train, bus, or car and then walk. Parking and public transport arrangements around the village change from season to season, so it's worth checking current timetables and parking rules close to your travel date rather than relying on older information. The shoreline and waterfall trails start close to the village, while the Salzberg and Krippenstein routes begin with a cable car, whose opening hours and last-descent times are seasonal — check them locally before you commit to a late departure.

If you're staying in the village overnight, you'll have a real advantage: you can start walking early, before the day-trip crowds arrive, and see the trails at their quietest. If you're visiting for the day, aim to be on the path by mid-morning at the latest, and keep an eye on your return transport so a longer hike doesn't leave you stranded.

What to Pack for a Beginner Hike

  • Footwear: comfortable trail shoes or light hiking boots with good grip. Surfaces can be damp or uneven even on easy trails.
  • Layers: a light waterproof jacket and a warm mid-layer. It's often noticeably cooler and windier up high than down by the lake.
  • Water and snacks: more than you think you need, especially on the moderate trails where there may be no place to buy anything.
  • Sun protection: a hat, sunglasses, and sunscreen. Alpine sun is strong even on cool days.
  • Phone and power bank: for photos, maps, and emergencies. Downloading an offline map before you go is a sensible habit, as covered in our guide to using Google Maps offline.
  • Small first-aid kit: plasters for blisters are the most useful item by far.

Weather and Safety in the Alps

Mountain weather can change faster than most beginners expect. A bright morning can turn cloudy, windy, or wet by the afternoon, so check the forecast before you set off, look at the sky as you climb, and be willing to turn around if conditions change. Starting early gives you the most margin, since it leaves daylight to spare if you need to slow down or take a longer route back.

Stay on marked paths, follow the trail signs, and don't shortcut across steep or wet ground. Tell someone where you're going and roughly when you expect to be back, particularly if you're hiking alone. Phone signal can be patchy in valleys and on exposed ridges, so it's wise to know the local emergency number and to carry enough water and a warm layer to cope with an unexpected delay. None of this makes beginner trails dangerous — it simply means treating them with the same respect you'd give any mountain walk.

A Simple 3-Day Plan

If you have a few days in the area, the trails above fit together naturally, building from gentle to more demanding so your legs and confidence grow as you go:

  • Day 1 — Settle in: walk the Lake Hallstatt Shoreline Path in the afternoon, then add Waldbachstrub if you still have energy. Finish with a lakeside café stop.
  • Day 2 — Go up: take the cable car to the Salzberg Skywalk loop, then continue along the Echernwand Ridge Path if the weather is clear and you feel good.
  • Day 3 — The big day: tackle the Krippenstein Plateau Trail with an early start, plenty of water, and a firm turnaround time.

If the weather turns on one of the days, simply swap the order — the shoreline and waterfall walks make good rainy-day fallbacks, and the high trails can wait for the next clear window.

Trail Etiquette and Leave No Trace

Hallstatt's popularity has put real pressure on the village and surrounding trails, so small habits make a genuine difference. Carry your rubbish out with you, stay on marked paths to protect fragile ground, keep noise low so you don't disturb wildlife or other walkers, and close any gates you open, since livestock graze in some areas. Greet the people you pass — a friendly "Grüß Gott" is the usual alpine greeting — and give way to anyone coming uphill on narrow stretches. Respecting the place is also the best way to make sure it stays worth visiting.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need hiking experience for these trails?

No — all five are graded easy to moderate, well-marked, and manageable in normal trail shoes without technical gear.

What's the best time of year to hike near Hallstatt?

Late May through early October, once snow has cleared from the higher trails. June and September strike the best balance of open trails and thinner crowds.

Can I avoid the Hallstatt tourist crowds while hiking?

Yes — nearly all of these trails start a short walk or bus ride from the village center and see a fraction of the foot traffic of the lakeside viewpoint.

Do I need a cable car ticket for any of these?

Only for the Salzberg and Krippenstein routes, which use the Hallstatt cable car for the initial ascent before the walking trail begins.

What shoes should I wear on these trails?

Comfortable trail shoes or light hiking boots with good grip are ideal, especially for the moderate trails, where surfaces can be uneven or damp.

Is it safe to hike near Hallstatt alone?

The easier trails are well-marked and popular, so many beginners hike them solo, but it's sensible to check the weather, tell someone your plan, and carry water and a charged phone.
