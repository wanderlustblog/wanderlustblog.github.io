Google Reviews vs. Local Recommendations: Which Should You Trust?

August 21, 20269 min readby WanderLust Blog

Google reviews are fast, everywhere, and free. But after a decade of trips built entirely around 4.5-star ratings, we've learned they don't always lead you to the best places — just the most-reviewed ones.

Every trip we've taken in the last few years has started the same way: open Google Maps, search "best restaurant near me," and sort by rating. It's efficient, it's instant, and more often than not, it lands us somewhere perfectly fine — clean, safe, easy to find, exactly what the photos promised. It's also, we've slowly realized, rarely where we remember the trip actually happening.

Why Google reviews steer you toward the average

A rating system built on volume rewards the places that are easiest to find and easiest to review — spots near main roads, with English menus, with staff used to tourists leaving a star rating on their way out. The tiny noodle stall with no signage, run by someone who's never heard of Google Maps, simply can't compete on a metric that requires hundreds of digital reviews to look "trustworthy." It's not that Google reviews are wrong; it's that they measure popularity with a specific kind of visitor, not quality in any deeper sense.

We noticed this most clearly in Tokyo's quieter neighbourhoods, where some of the best small izakayas we found had barely a dozen reviews — not because they were bad, but because most of their regulars were locals who'd never think to rate a place they've eaten at for twenty years.

Where Google reviews genuinely earn their place

None of this means you should ignore them. For practical logistics — is this place actually open today, does this hotel have working hot water, is this trail currently accessible — reviews are still one of the fastest sanity checks available, especially in places where information changes often and official sources lag behind. Google's own travel planning tools, including Google Travel, pull in this kind of live data well, and we still use them for flight tracking, basic itinerary logistics, and spotting red flags before booking somewhere unfamiliar.

The distinction we've settled on: use Google reviews to rule places out, not to pick the winner. If a place has dozens of recent one-star reviews describing food poisoning or a scam, that's real signal worth heeding. But among the remaining options, the rating differences between 4.2 and 4.6 stars tell you almost nothing about which one you'll actually love.

How locals actually find the good places

The best recommendations we've ever received didn't come from a screen. They came from a guesthouse owner in rural Bali who drew us a hand map to a waterfall with no name, a tuk-tuk driver in Sri Lanka who insisted on taking us somewhere "better than what's on your phone," and a shopkeeper in Marrakech who walked us three streets over to his cousin's stall instead of the one we'd asked directions to.

These recommendations work because the person making them has no algorithm to satisfy — just a genuine sense of what's actually good, paired with an interest in whether you enjoy your visit. Asking is simple, if slightly uncomfortable for anyone used to researching everything in advance: "Where would you eat if it were your day off?" tends to get a far more honest answer than "what's the best restaurant here?"

A practical system for blending both

What's worked for us on recent trips is a two-layer approach. Before arriving, we use Google reviews purely as a safety net — confirming a neighbourhood is generally fine, a hotel isn't a scam, a trail is currently open. Once we're actually on the ground, we stop consulting the app for "best of" lists entirely and start asking people directly: hosts, drivers, other travelers who've been in town a few days longer than us.

This mirrors the slow travel philosophy we've written about before — the places worth remembering are rarely the ones with the most reviews, but the ones someone cared enough to walk you to personally.

A simple test before you trust a rating

If a place has more than a few hundred reviews and sits directly on the busiest tourist street in town, treat the rating as a measure of foot traffic more than quality. If a place has a handful of reviews, all glowing, written in a mix of languages by people who clearly weren't part of a tour group — that's often a better signal than a polished four-and-a-half stars with thousands of ratings.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are Google reviews reliable for travel decisions?

They're a reasonable starting filter for logistics like hours and safety, but ratings skew toward tourist-heavy spots and can miss smaller places locals actually prefer.

How do I find local recommendations while traveling?

Ask guesthouse staff, taxi and rickshaw drivers, and shop owners directly, and look for places with almost no English signage or online presence at all.

Should I stop using Google reviews entirely?

No — they're still useful for practical checks like current opening hours and general safety, just not as the final word on where to eat or what to see.
