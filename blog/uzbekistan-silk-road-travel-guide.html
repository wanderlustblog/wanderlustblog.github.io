Uzbekistan: The Silk Road Country Tourism Forgot

August 15, 202612 min readWanderLust Blog

Turquoise domes, madrasas older than most European cathedrals, and a country that spent centuries at the center of world trade — and somehow still isn't on most people's radar.

Uzbekistan sat at the literal crossroads of the historic Silk Road for centuries, and its cities still show it — Samarkand, Bukhara, and Khiva each built around monumental Islamic architecture that predates most of what draws crowds in Western Europe, and yet see a fraction of the visitors.

Registan Square, in person

Photographs of Samarkand's Registan Square are common enough online, but none of them quite prepare you for standing in the middle of it — three enormous madrasas facing each other across an open square, their facades covered in turquoise and blue tilework that catches the light differently depending on the hour. We arrived at opening time specifically to avoid both the heat and the tour groups, and had long stretches of the square almost entirely to ourselves.

Bukhara's living old town

Unlike Samarkand, where the major monuments sit somewhat apart from daily city life, Bukhara's old town still functions as an actual neighborhood — artisans working in small shops beneath centuries-old domed trading halls, tea houses tucked into courtyards, a sense that the historic center never fully stopped being used the way it was originally built for. We spent an entire afternoon simply wandering without a fixed plan and found it more rewarding than any single monument visit.

Khiva, the walled city

Getting to Khiva takes more effort than the other two cities — it sits further west, reached by either a considerably longer train journey or a domestic flight — but the old walled city inside, Itchan Kala, is compact enough to fully explore on foot and dense with preserved architecture in a way that felt distinct from both Samarkand and Bukhara.

Food worth seeking out

Plov, the national dish built around rice, meat, and carrots slow-cooked together, varies noticeably by region and is worth trying in more than one city to catch the local variations. Street-side chaikhanas (tea houses) serve it alongside fresh bread baked in clay tandir ovens, and became our default lunch stop most days.

The high-speed Afrosiyob train between Tashkent, Samarkand, and Bukhara is comfortable, efficient, and dramatically faster than the region's reputation might suggest — book a few days ahead in peak season.

What surprised us most

How relaxed and modern Tashkent itself felt compared to the more historic cities further west — wide Soviet-era boulevards, an efficient metro system with individually decorated stations worth riding just to see, and a food scene that blends Uzbek, Russian, and Korean influences in a way that reflects the city's layered history.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a visa to visit Uzbekistan?

Uzbekistan has significantly relaxed its visa policy in recent years, with visa-free entry for many nationalities — check current requirements for your passport before booking, since policy can change.

Is Uzbekistan safe for travelers?

Yes, generally — it's considered one of the safer countries in Central Asia for visitors, with low rates of violent crime against tourists.

How do you get between Samarkand, Bukhara, and Khiva?

A high-speed train connects Tashkent, Samarkand, and Bukhara efficiently; reaching Khiva typically requires either a longer train journey or a domestic flight.

What's the best season to visit?

Spring (April–May) and autumn (September–October) avoid the extreme summer heat and winter cold that Uzbekistan's continental climate produces.
