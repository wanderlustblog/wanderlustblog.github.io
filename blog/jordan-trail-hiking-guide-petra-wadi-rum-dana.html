Jordan Trail Hiking Guide for Beginners: Dana to Petra, Wadi Rum, Season and Planning Tips

November 13, 202613 min readby WanderLust Blog

Most visitors see Jordan from a coach, but a footpath crosses the whole country from the Roman ruins of the north to the Red Sea, passing villages, canyons, desert and the rose-red city of Petra.

The Jordan Trail is a long-distance walking route that runs for roughly 650 kilometres from Umm Qais, near the borders with Syria and Israel in the north, to Aqaba on the Red Sea in the south. It crosses a wide variety of landscapes, from olive groves and pine forests to deep canyons, high plateaus, red desert and the sandstone mountains of Wadi Rum. Along the way it passes Roman and Nabataean ruins, castles, Bedouin camps and village homes, and ends at the coast. Most travellers walk only a section, and the most popular for first-timers is the multi-day route from the Dana Biosphere Reserve to Petra. Jordan itself is often listed as an under-the-radar adventure destination compared with the busy Petra day tours. This guide explains how the trail works, which sections suit beginners, the best season, safety and logistics, and how to plan a trip.

Why the Jordan Trail Is a Hidden Location

Every year, many travellers visit Petra and Wadi Rum on short tours, but far fewer walk through the landscapes between them. Walking changes the experience: you meet shepherds, drink tea in Bedouin tents and see views that cannot be reached by road. It is also one of the safest ways to experience rural Jordan, with strong local hospitality. At the same time, it is a desert and mountain environment, with heat, little water and weather risks, so planning and local support matter.

Understanding the Route

The trail is divided into several sections, each with its own character.

  • The north: from Umm Qais, with views of the Sea of Galilee and the Golan Heights, through the green hills of Ajloun, with forests, castles and villages.
  • The centre: through the Dead Sea highlands, canyons and the area near Madaba and Mount Nebo.
  • Dana to Petra: a high-demand section of about 75 kilometres, crossing the Dana Biosphere Reserve and the hills toward Petra, often walked in about six days.
  • Petra to Wadi Rum: a journey through desert landscapes to the sandstone mountains and red sand of Wadi Rum.
  • Wadi Rum to Aqaba: the final stretch, ending at the Red Sea.

The complete route takes around five to six weeks. Most visitors choose a section or two, and use local guides and support services.

A Beginner-Friendly Section: Dana to Petra

The Dana to Petra route is popular because of its scenery and its mixture of villages, canyon walking, desert plains and the dramatic arrival at Petra by a back route. A typical schedule takes about five to six days, with overnight stays in Bedouin camps or guesthouses and daily distances of around 12 to 20 kilometres. It is demanding because of heat, terrain and lack of water, so it needs fitness, preparation and a guide or an experienced local support team. Some days include rocky paths, steep descents and sections with no shade.

Petra and Wadi Rum Without a Long Trek

If you do not want to walk for several days, you can still experience parts of the trail on short hikes. In Petra, trails lead to high places such as the Monastery and the viewpoint over the Treasury, and routes through the back valleys that avoid the main crowds. In Wadi Rum, guided half-day and full-day walks reveal rock bridges, canyons and Bedouin life, and some operators combine walking with jeep transport. A three-day plan could include a day in Petra, a night in Wadi Rum and a day of walking with a Bedouin guide.

Best Time to Hike

  • March to May (spring): mild temperatures, wildflowers and long days. This is the most popular period, and ideal for walking, though there may be rain in the early part.
  • September to November (autumn): warm, clear and generally dry, with fewer crowds than spring.
  • June to August (summer): very hot, especially in the south and the desert. Hiking is not recommended for beginners, and water supply is a real risk.
  • December to February (winter): cold and wet, with possible snow in the highlands and flash floods in canyons. Some sections may be difficult.

Check the weather forecast, and avoid canyons and wadis if rain is expected, since flash floods can be dangerous.

Guides, Support and Safety

Many walkers hire a local guide, particularly for first-time desert hikes and for sections where navigation is difficult. A guide knows the water sources, the weather and the people, and ensures the walk supports local families. Hiring a guide, with a driver or luggage transport, also lets you carry a lighter pack. If you walk independently, download the official route data, carry a map and a GPS device, tell someone your plan, and carry enough water for the section. Do not rely on mobile coverage. Follow the advice of Bedouin hosts and park staff.

  • Carry at least three litres of water per person per day in warm weather, and know the next refill point.
  • Wear a hat, sunglasses, sunscreen and clothing that covers your skin.
  • Do not enter canyons in rain, and watch the sky.
  • Carry a first-aid kit, a head torch and a power bank.
  • Respect local customs, particularly regarding dress and photography.

The Jordan Pass and Entry Rules

Entry rules for Jordan depend on your nationality, and Indian travellers should check the official Jordanian embassy or government website for visa requirements before booking. Many visitors buy the Jordan Pass, an official tourist pass that bundles entry to Petra and many other sites and, for those who stay for at least three nights, can include the visa fee. Check the current terms, since prices and conditions change, and make sure the pass is purchased from the official source. Where to Stay and What to Eat

Along the trail, accommodation includes Bedouin camps, guesthouses, eco-lodges and small hotels, with meals often included. Typical meals include flatbread, hummus, falafel, grilled meat, lentil soup and mint tea. Vegetarian options are common, since many dishes are vegetable-based. In Bedouin camps, expect simple tents and shared facilities. In Petra and Wadi Rum, there is a wide range of hotels and camps. Book ahead in spring and autumn, and tell your host about dietary needs.

A 7-Day Beginner Plan

Days 1 to 2: Amman and the road south

Arrive in Amman, see the city, and travel along the King's Highway to Dana, stopping at sites such as Mount Nebo or Karak Castle.

Days 3 to 4: Dana walking

Walk in the Dana Biosphere Reserve with a guide, enjoying the views, villages and an overnight stay in a village guesthouse or camp.

Days 5 to 6: Petra

Walk toward Petra, or travel by vehicle if you prefer, and spend a full day exploring Petra's tombs and high places. Arrive early to avoid the crowds and the midday heat.

Day 7: Wadi Rum or return

Add a night in Wadi Rum for desert scenery, or return to Amman with a buffer for your flight.

Packing List

  • Broken-in hiking boots and spare socks, and sandals for camp.
  • Lightweight, long-sleeved layers, a wide-brimmed hat and sunglasses.
  • A warm layer for cold nights, a rain jacket in winter and a sleeping bag liner.
  • A hydration system or large water bottles, electrolyte tablets and snacks.
  • Navigation tools, a power bank, head torch and first-aid kit.
  • A scarf for dust and modest dress, and a small daypack.

Responsible and Respectful Travel

Treat the trail as a living landscape. Stay on paths, do not litter, buy local products and pay fair prices for guides and hospitality. Dress modestly in villages, ask before photographing people and accept hospitality with thanks. Respect archaeological sites and do not climb on ruins. Choose operators that support local communities and treat animals well, including camels and horses.

Budget Planning Without Guesswork

Costs vary by season, guide, accommodation and group size. Build your estimate from flights to Amman, transfers, guide and support fees, accommodation and meals, entry fees or a Jordan Pass, insurance, a visa if needed and a buffer of 10 to 15 percent. Compare quotes from several local operators, and ask what is included.

More Questions Travellers Ask

Can beginners hike the Jordan Trail?

Yes, on selected sections with a guide, good preparation and attention to heat and water. The full trail is a major undertaking.

Do I need a guide?

Not always, but a guide is strongly recommended for first-timers, especially in desert and canyon areas.

Is Jordan safe for tourists?

Jordan is generally considered welcoming to visitors, but check current government travel advice, especially for areas near borders.

How many days do I need?

A week gives you Petra, Wadi Rum and a few days of walking, and two weeks lets you do a longer section.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Hiking in summer heat without enough water.
  • Entering canyons when rain is forecast.
  • Underestimating the distances and terrain.
  • Not checking entry rules, insurance and permit requirements.
  • Skipping a local guide on difficult sections.

Final Checklist

  • Season, section and guide confirmed.
  • Entry rules, Jordan Pass and insurance arranged.
  • Boots, hat, water system and navigation tools packed.
  • A flexible schedule, with a spare day for weather or heat.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long is the Jordan Trail?

About 650 km from Umm Qais in the north to Aqaba on the Red Sea.

What is the best section for beginners?

Dana to Petra, around 75 km and usually five to six days, with a guide.

When is the best time to hike?

March to May and September to November. Summer is very hot and winter can be cold and wet.

Do I need a guide for the Jordan Trail?

Not always, but a guide is strongly recommended for first-timers, especially in the desert and canyons.

What is the Jordan Pass?

An official tourist pass that bundles entry to Petra and many other sites and may include the visa fee for longer stays. Check current terms.

Is hiking in Jordan safe?

With good preparation, a guide and attention to weather and water, yes. Check government travel advice.
