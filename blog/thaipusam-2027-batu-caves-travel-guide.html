Thaipusam 2027 at Batu Caves: Date, Procession, What to Expect and Visitor Tips

October 28, 202612 min readby WanderLust Blog

Thaipusam is one of the most intense religious festivals in Asia, and the Batu Caves procession is both a deeply sacred devotion and a spectacle that tests visitors' preparation and respect.

Thaipusam is a Hindu festival, celebrated mainly by the Tamil community, in honour of Lord Murugan, the god of war and victory. It falls on the full moon in the Tamil month of Thai, and it is marked with processions, offerings and acts of devotion and penance. The largest celebration outside India takes place at the Batu Caves in Selangor, near Kuala Lumpur, where hundreds of thousands of devotees and visitors gather. For visitors, it can be a moving and unforgettable experience, but it is also crowded, hot and religious, so it requires a respectful and well-prepared approach. This guide explains the date, what happens, how to reach Batu Caves, how to behave and what to wear.

Thaipusam 2027 Date

According to the Hindu calendar, Thaipusam in 2027 falls on Friday, January 22. Because the festival follows the lunar calendar, the date shifts each year, and exact timings of the main events depend on the star and moon cycles. Check with local temples and Malaysian tourism sources close to the date. In Malaysia, Thaipusam is a public holiday in Kuala Lumpur and several states, so expect closures, traffic and crowded public transport. The main celebrations begin the night before and continue through the day.

What Happens at Batu Caves

  • The overnight procession: on the eve of Thaipusam, a silver chariot carrying the statue of Lord Murugan travels in a long procession from a temple in central Kuala Lumpur to Batu Caves, accompanied by devotees, music and chanting.
  • The climb: at dawn and through the day, devotees climb the 272 steps to the main cave temple, many carrying offerings.
  • Paal kudam: many devotees carry pots of milk as an offering, balanced on their heads and walking barefoot.
  • Kavadi: some carry kavadi, elaborate decorated frames, and some take part in forms of penance, such as piercings with hooks and skewers, as a sign of devotion and thanks. This can be difficult for some visitors to watch.
  • The atmosphere: drums, chanting, bright colours and large crowds of families and groups, in a mix of devotion and celebration.

Batu Caves in Brief

Batu Caves is a limestone hill with a series of cave temples, located about 13 km north of central Kuala Lumpur. A huge golden statue of Murugan, about 43 metres tall, stands at the entrance, and the long, colourful staircase leads up to the main Temple Cave. The site is also home to many macaque monkeys, which are used to snatching food and small items, so keep your belongings secure and do not carry loose food.

How to Get to Batu Caves on Thaipusam

The easiest way is the KTM Komuter train from Kuala Lumpur Sentral to Batu Caves station, which operates extra services around the festival. Expect long queues and crowded carriages, so go very early or late. Taxis and ride-hailing services may be restricted or very expensive, and roads close near the site. Plan to arrive well before dawn if you want to see the main climb, and allow plenty of time for the return. Keep small change for transport, and confirm timetables from official sources.

A Plan for Visiting as an Observer

The night before

Stay in Kuala Lumpur, and, if you wish, see the start of the chariot procession from a safe spot, then rest.

Early morning

Take the train to Batu Caves before sunrise. Choose a viewing position away from the narrowest points, and watch the devotees arrive. Stay in the areas marked for visitors.

Late morning

Leave before the heat and the biggest crowds build, or have a break in a shaded area. If you want to climb the steps, remember that the staircase is reserved for devotees at peak times.

Etiquette: How to Be Respectful

  • Dress modestly: cover shoulders and knees, and avoid tight or revealing clothing. Light cotton clothes work best in the heat. Wearing white or bright colours is common, but avoid black, which is not usually worn on festive days.
  • Remove shoes before entering temple areas, and do not point your feet at religious objects.
  • Ask before photographing: do not photograph people in trance or in distress, and avoid close-ups of piercings. Follow instructions from volunteers and police, and keep your camera discreet.
  • Do not touch devotees, kavadi or offerings, and do not block the procession.
  • Be vegetarian for the day: many devotees fast and eat only vegetarian food, so avoid eating meat or drinking alcohol near the temple areas.

Health, Heat and Crowd Safety

Malaysia is hot and humid, and the crowds make it hotter. Carry water, a hat or umbrella, sunscreen and wet wipes. Wear comfortable, closed shoes that you can remove easily, and consider old shoes you do not mind losing. Keep valuables in zipped pockets, avoid carrying large bags and arrange a meeting point in case of separation. If you feel dizzy or unwell, leave the crowd and find help. Check health advice and consider travel insurance.

Where to Stay

Stay in central Kuala Lumpur near a train line, such as around KL Sentral or Bukit Bintang. Book early, since January is a busy time and hotels sell out in the week of Thaipusam. Check cancellation terms and ask your hotel about transport and early check-in or late checkout options.

Food and Drink

Around Batu Caves, stalls sell vegetarian food, snacks, fruit and drinks, and local restaurants in nearby Little India serve South Indian meals. Try dosa, banana leaf rice and masala tea. Eat at busy places and drink bottled water. If you have allergies, ask about ingredients.

Other Places to See Thaipusam

Thaipusam is also celebrated in Penang, Ipoh and Singapore, with different atmospheres. Singapore's procession runs between two temples through the city, and is more organised and smaller in scale than Batu Caves. If you are comparing destinations, read our Singapore guide for logistics and weather.

More Questions Visitors Ask

Can tourists attend Thaipusam?

Yes, as respectful observers. Follow local rules and the instructions of organisers.

Is it safe?

It is generally safe, but crowds, heat and the intensity of some rituals can be challenging. Take basic precautions and stay with a group.

Is it suitable for children?

It can be overwhelming for young children because of the crowds, the noise and some of the rituals. Consider whether it is appropriate for your family.

How many days do I need in Kuala Lumpur?

Two to three days gives time for Thaipusam and other highlights.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Arriving late and getting stuck in the crowds.
  • Photographing devotees intrusively.
  • Wearing revealing clothing or leather items in religious areas.
  • Carrying food openly near the monkeys.

Final Checklist

  • Date and timings confirmed locally.
  • Hotel near a train line booked with flexible terms.
  • Modest, light clothing, water, sunscreen and a power bank packed.
  • A plan for an early start, a meeting point and a safe return.

A Note on Watching Intense Rituals

Some parts of Thaipusam involve acts of penance that visitors may find confronting, including piercings and long barefoot walks carried out in a state of deep devotion. Devotees prepare for weeks with fasting, prayer and discipline, and the acts are an expression of faith and thanks, not entertainment. Observe quietly, keep a respectful distance, do not comment loudly or laugh, and step away if you feel uncomfortable. If you wish to learn more, read about the festival's meaning beforehand or visit a temple in Kuala Lumpur to speak with volunteers.

Where to Go After Thaipusam

Kuala Lumpur makes an easy base for the rest of your trip. Spend an afternoon in Little India's Brickfields, see the Petronas Towers and the National Mosque, and try a hawker centre for local food. Penang and Malacca are a few hours away by road or train, and add heritage streets and food. Keep a rest day after Thaipusam, since the early start and the heat are tiring.
