If you have ever sent a pitch and heard nothing back, you are in good company. Most travel blog inboxes fill up quickly, and a surprising number of pitches look identical: a vague compliment, a request to "collaborate" and a link to a site that has nothing to do with travel. At WanderLust we see that pattern all the time, and it is the main reason good ideas get lost.

This guide is the one we wish every pitcher had read first. It explains what travel blog guest post opportunities really are, where to find the ones worth your time, how to judge a blog before you write a word, and how to pitch in a way that makes an editor want to say yes. It also covers what we offer here, including our prices, so there are no surprises. Whether you are a writer building a portfolio or a business that wants to reach travellers, you should leave with a plan you can start today.

What Counts as a Travel Blog Guest Post Opportunity?

A guest post is an article written by someone who does not run the blog it appears on. In travel, that can mean several different things, and it helps to know which one you are really after because each works differently. Broadly, you will come across three main types, and a few smaller formats around them.

  • Editorial guest posts. The editor picks your idea because it will help or interest readers. You get a byline, a short author bio and sometimes a link to your own site or social profile. Money rarely changes hands.
  • Sponsored guest posts. A brand or business pays for a full article to be published. A reputable blog labels these clearly so readers know what they are looking at.
  • Link insertions. A relevant link to your page is added inside an existing post where it genuinely fits. If it is paid, it should be labelled as such.
  • Smaller formats. Expert quotes, roundups, joint itineraries, interviews and "ask the local" features. They take less time and can be a gentle way to start a relationship with an editor.

Why would a blogger say yes to any of this? Usually because travel is too big for one person. No single writer has been everywhere, speaks every language or knows every neighbourhood. A guest who has actually lived the experience can add detail the host cannot, and fresh voices keep a blog from sounding repetitive. That is the opening you are trying to step into.

Who Benefits Most From Guest Posting on a Travel Blog?

Almost anyone with real knowledge of travel can benefit, but the payoff looks different depending on who you are. Here are the four groups we hear from most often.

Writers and aspiring bloggers

A guest post gives you a published clip, a byline and a way to be read by people who do not know you yet. If you are building a portfolio, it is one of the quickest ways to prove you can write something readers finish.

Tour operators and agencies

You know your routes better than anyone. A practical piece, such as a first-timer's guide to a region you run trips in, puts that knowledge in front of travellers who are already planning. Our advice on choosing an international travel agency from India shows the kind of angle readers appreciate.

Hotels, homestays and local guides

Local detail is what readers trust. A well-told piece about your neighbourhood, written for travellers rather than as an advert, tends to earn far more goodwill than a brochure ever will.

Visa, insurance and travel-service businesses

Travellers search for help long before they book. Clear explainers about paperwork and cover, like our guide to visa-free countries for Indian passport holders and our travel insurance guide for Indians, sit naturally on a travel blog.

Where to Find Travel Blog Guest Post Opportunities

The best opportunities rarely announce themselves with a flashing sign. Here are seven places to look, roughly in the order we would try them.

  1. Start with blogs you already read. You know their tone, their readers and their gaps. A pitch from a genuine reader is almost always warmer than one from a stranger running through a list.
  2. Search with intent. Combine your topic with phrases such as write for us, guest post guidelines, submit a guest post or contribute, then add words like travel, visa, itinerary or budget. Search in a private window so your own history does not skew the results.
  3. Follow the bylines. When a blog already publishes guests, check who they are and where else those writers appear. Every byline is a small map to more blogs with similar tastes.
  4. Read the "work with us" pages. Many blogs describe exactly what they want on their contact, about or advertise pages. Our own about page and contact page are good examples to compare with.
  5. Join travel writing communities. Forums, Facebook groups and Slack or Discord communities often share calls for pitches, along with honest feedback about which editors are pleasant to work with.
  6. Use social media sensibly. Editors post calls for contributors on X, LinkedIn, Bluesky, Mastodon and Instagram. Reply with something useful before you ever send a direct message.
  7. Look at newsletters and tourism boards. Newsletter writers and destination marketing teams often know which blogs are open to outside voices, and sometimes run their own contributor programmes.

How to Judge a Travel Blog Before You Pitch

A published guest post is only as valuable as the place it appears. Before you invest hours in writing, spend ten minutes checking the blog itself. Here is what we look at when deciding whether a site is worth our name, and what you should look at too.

  • Original photos and first-hand detail. If every image looks like a stock photo and every post could have been written without leaving home, walk away.
  • Recent, regular publishing. A blog that last posted two years ago is unlikely to send readers your way.
  • A clear topic focus. Travel blogs that also publish posts about loans, casinos or unrelated products are a warning sign.
  • Real people behind it. Look for an about page, named authors and a working contact method.
  • Signs of a real audience. Comments, shares, an active newsletter or social following all help, though none is perfect on its own.
  • Visibility in Google. Type the blog's address into Google with site: in front of it. If few or no pages appear, the site may have problems.
  • Honest labelling. Sponsored content should say so. A blog that hides paid posts is not one you want your name next to.
A quick sanity testAsk yourself whether you would be proud to show this blog to a client or a friend. If the answer is no, the link or byline is not worth having, however easy the placement looks.

How to Pitch a Travel Blog, Step by Step

Pitching is a small skill that most people never practise. The good news is that it is learnable, and following these six steps puts you ahead of the majority of pitches an editor sees.

  1. Read the blog properly. Spend at least twenty minutes on recent posts. Notice the tone, the length, the way headings are written and the topics already covered. Never pitch something the blog published last month.
  2. Find the gap. Look for a question readers would ask that the blog has not answered, a destination it has not covered or a viewpoint it lacks. Your idea should fill a hole, not repeat a post.
  3. Write a short, specific pitch. Three to five sentences is plenty. State your working title, what the article will cover, why it helps this blog's readers and why you are the right person to write it.
  4. Show proof you can write. Link to two or three samples, ideally on travel, and keep the links clean. If you have no published work, write a short sample on your own page first.
  5. Follow up once, politely. If you hear nothing after about a week or two, send one short, friendly reminder. Then move on. Repeated chasing closes doors.
  6. Say thank you and promote the post. If the piece goes live, share it, answer comments and thank the editor. People remember who made their job easier, and that is how one post turns into several.

A Pitch Email You Can Adapt

Templates are not magic, but a good structure saves you from rambling. Copy the one below, then change every bracket to something specific. A pitch that sounds like it was written for one blog will always beat one that sounds written for all of them.

Subject: Guest post idea: [specific working title]

Hi [Editor's name],

I read your guide to [specific post] and especially liked [one genuine detail]. It made me think of something your readers might want next.

I'd like to pitch: "[Working title]". It would cover:
- [Point one that answers a real reader question]
- [Point two with a first-hand detail]
- [Point three, such as costs, mistakes or timing]

Readers who are [type of traveller] would come away able to [clear benefit].

A little about me: [one or two lines about your real experience]. Here are two samples: [link one], [link two].

Would this suit the blog? I'm happy to adjust the angle or length.

Thank you for your time,
[Your name]
[Your website or profile]

How to Write a Guest Post Editors Actually Publish

Getting a yes is only half the job. The other half is delivering a piece that needs very little rework. Editors remember the writers who make their lives easier, and they come back to them. These habits make the difference.

  • Answer the question early. Tell readers what they will learn in the opening lines, then spend the rest of the article proving it.
  • Use first-hand detail. Real prices, real times, real mistakes and small surprises are what separate a useful guide from a generic one.
  • Write the way you speak, a little tidier. Short sentences, plain words and direct advice read better than formal, padded copy.
  • Structure for skimmers. Clear headings, short paragraphs and the occasional list help readers on phones find what they need.
  • Be honest about trade-offs. Readers trust writers who admit what was disappointing or expensive. Our Antarctica guide is a good example of balancing excitement with practical warnings.
  • Check your facts. Visa rules, opening hours and prices change. Confirm them with official sources and say when something may have changed.
  • Send original images you have the right to use. Add a short description for each so the editor can write accessible alt text.
  • Aim for a sensible length. Most editors are happy with roughly 1,000 to 2,000 words, but always check the blog's guidelines first.

Links, Anchor Text and Disclosure

Links are the part of guest posting that people worry about most, and the part where most mistakes happen. The principle is simple: a link should help the reader, not just the site it points to.

Aim for one or two links at most, and make sure each one points to a page that genuinely adds something to the sentence it sits in. Anchor text, the clickable words, should describe what the reader will find. "Our guide to travel insurance for Indians" tells people and search engines far more than "click here", and it is much safer than repeating the exact same keyword in every link. Stuffing keywords into anchors looks unnatural and can do more harm than good.

Where a link is paid or promotional, say so. Google asks that paid links carry the sponsored label, and readers appreciate honesty. We also avoid redirects and tracking links inside articles. A direct link to a useful page is cleaner for readers and for you.

Think of a link as a recommendationIf you would not happily recommend the page to a friend who asked a question, it probably does not belong in the post.

Mistakes That Sink Pitches

Most rejections are not about talent. They are about avoidable slips. Here are the ones we see most often.

  • Sending the same message to fifty blogs, with the editor's name misspelt or missing.
  • Pitching a topic the blog has already covered in depth.
  • Leading with what you want, a link or a payment, rather than what readers will gain.
  • Proposing a post with no real connection to travel.
  • Sending a finished article before anyone has asked for it, with no idea whether it suits the blog.
  • Insisting on exact-match anchor text or a long list of links.
  • Disappearing after the post goes live instead of sharing it and replying to comments.

How to Measure Whether a Guest Post Worked

A guest post is not a lottery ticket, so judge it with a clear head. Within the first few weeks, look at the traffic that arrives from the post, how long those visitors stay and whether they do anything useful, such as reading another page, signing up or sending an enquiry. Over a longer period, watch for people searching for your name or brand, links you earn from others and conversations that start because someone read your article.

Be patient. Trust takes time, and the biggest return from guest posting is often a relationship with an editor who thinks of you next time. Keep notes on what each post achieved so you can do more of what works and drop what does not. If a blog sends visitors who never engage, that tells you something useful about whether to write there again.

Final Thoughts

Travel blog guest post opportunities are everywhere, but the good ones go to people who do a little more homework than everyone else. Choose blogs you respect, pitch ideas that help readers, write with real detail, and treat every editor as a person rather than a link source. If you do those four things, your inbox will start to look very different.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are travel blog guest post opportunities?

They are chances to publish an article on another person's travel blog, usually in exchange for a byline, an author bio, a relevant link or access to a new audience. Some are editorial and free, some are sponsored and paid, and some blogs don't accept guest posts at all.

How do I find travel blogs that accept guest posts?

Start with blogs you already read, then search for phrases like write for us or guest post guidelines combined with travel topics. Check author bylines, work-with-us pages, travel writing communities and social media. Always read a few recent posts before pitching.

How much does a guest post on a travel blog cost?

It varies. Many editors publish strong editorial pitches at no charge, while sponsored placements are paid. At WanderLust, a sponsored guest post costs $100 and a link insertion in an existing relevant post costs $50. Email us to confirm current terms.

Do guest posts help SEO?

A link from a relevant, trusted site can help people and search engines find your page, but quality and relevance matter far more than quantity. Paid links should be marked with rel=sponsored. No blog can guarantee rankings, so treat a guest post as a way to reach readers first.

How long should a travel guest post be?

Most editors prefer between 1,000 and 2,000 words, long enough to be genuinely useful and short enough to stay focused. Check each blog's guidelines, because length and format preferences differ.

Can I put links in my guest post?

Usually one or two relevant links are fine, ideally to a page that genuinely helps readers. Use natural, descriptive anchor text, avoid stuffing keywords, and expect the editor to label or adjust paid or promotional links.
